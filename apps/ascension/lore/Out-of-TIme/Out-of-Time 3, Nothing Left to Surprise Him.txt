# Nothing Left to Surprise Him

Telepathy had learned early that a mind was easier to conquer than a body. A locked door kept someone outside. A strong enough wall stopped a bullet. Neither had ever once stopped a thought.

He was young, as the Ascended measured such things, and already past most people who had spent decades refining what he'd been born knowing how to do — speak without a voice, enter a stranger's thinking uninvited, bend perception, drag a buried memory to the surface with nothing sharper than attention. He rarely needed to threaten anyone directly. Fear did that work for him, and fear was always already sitting somewhere in a person's head, waiting to be pointed at something useful. Regret. Shame. The memory of someone they hadn't managed to save.

He built an organization around that knowledge. The Ascended Private Security Company began as exactly what its name promised — protection, sold to governments, corporations, and the individually wealthy who could afford it. Under his particular kind of guidance, it grew into something considerably larger than protection. Anyone who investigated APSC too closely eventually met someone who could erase what they'd seen, reshape what a witness remembered, or convince an investigator that the trail they'd been following had simply run cold.

The company became close to unchallengeable. And then, with nothing left to threaten him and no puzzle left worth the effort of paperwork, Telepathy became bored.

### The trials

Boredom, in a man built the way he was, produced projects. APSC would create Ascendants rather than simply employ the ones the world already had.

The first six trials ended in death, each one differently, none of them cleanly. Telepathy was listed among the experimental subjects on paper, in a record built specifically to be believed by everyone except the two people who knew it was false — himself, and the staff quietly instructed to replace his injections with something harmless. On the seventh attempt, the serum finally did what it was designed to do, and a human being survived the process carrying an ability that had not existed in anyone before them.

Then another did. The Synthetic Ascendants had arrived, unstable and occasionally, usefully, powerful enough to break containment on their own.

That last quality interested Telepathy more than any other. APSC began releasing selected subjects deliberately, letting the public believe they were witnessing dangerous experiments running loose, letting the same company that created the danger sell the solution to it. Every trial, successful or not, made APSC richer. Whenever a subject proved capable of escaping cleanly, Telepathy made certain they did, tracked them down afterward at his leisure, and offered the same choice every time: join, or don't survive the alternative for very long.

Most joined.

### The man who couldn't be killed twice

A story eventually spread that a Synthetic Ascendant had killed him during a containment operation — no body, no confirmation, only witnesses swearing they'd seen it happen. APSC denied the rumor without much conviction, and the next morning Telepathy walked back into headquarters and demonstrated, in front of a room that had genuinely believed him dead, exactly how he'd survived.

He created another version of himself. It looked like him, thought independently of him, spoke in his voice without needing him present to borrow it, and for the first few seconds even the people watching couldn't have said with certainty which one was original.

From then on, the duplicate did the dying for him. It took the field assignments too dangerous to risk his own continuity on. If it was injured, it could be replaced. If it was destroyed, another could be built to take its place, and after a while, no one at APSC bothered asking which Telepathy they were speaking to, because there had stopped being a practical reason to know the difference.

### Trial twenty-three

The twenty-third trial looked routine until, against every pattern the previous twenty-two had established, a subject survived alone. Her name, before the experiment, had been Reign. She chose Telekinesis afterward, and Telepathy's duplicate met her in the wreckage of her own escape and offered her exactly what he offered everyone useful: a place, a purpose, a reason not to keep running.

She accepted. Something about her survival, though, sat wrong enough in the original Telepathy's calculations that he did what he had never bothered doing for any subject before her — he reached across the distance and took direct control of his own duplicate, stepping into her presence in a body that wasn't strictly his to examine her more closely than the file had allowed.

He went through her memories carefully. The bombing. The friend who'd vanished from a security recording that shouldn't have shown anything at all. The three years of grief that had curdled into obsession and then into a warehouse she should never have found. He found nothing that explained her survival. Her telekinesis was unremarkable by the standard the trials had already set. Her body carried the same synthetic markers as every other success. She wasn't hiding a second ability, and she wasn't secretly stronger than anyone else who'd lived.

She had simply lived, for reasons the data didn't contain.

He found that more interesting than almost anything the trials had produced in years, and released the duplicate without ever telling her whose attention had briefly occupied the body across from her.

### What she never knew

When Reign eventually asked why Telepathy himself had taken an interest in a single trial subject, he gave her an answer built to be believed — that he'd been investigating the experiments from the inside for reasons of his own, hunting whoever was truly behind them. She believed it easily, because she had her own investigation running in parallel and needed his resources exactly as much as he apparently needed hers.

He placed her in the Intelligence Branch without hesitation. She took the position gladly, certain APSC's archives would finally surface something about Ella that three years of ordinary searching hadn't. She did not know the company she'd joined had ordered the very trial that made her what she now was. She did not know the man guiding her research had already looked inside her mind once and found her wanting only in the sense of being unexplained. And she did not know that somewhere behind the arrangement she believed was mutual convenience, she was being allowed to search precisely because whatever she found, APSC would learn of it first — and whatever danger she uncovered would already know exactly where to find her.

### Something briefly missing

Months into their work together, Reign kept one object from before the bombing — a small carved token Ella had given her years earlier, the only physical thing she still had that had touched her friend's actual hands rather than a file about her. She noticed one evening that it wasn't where she'd left it. She found it again the next morning, in the same drawer, in the same condition, except that a scratch along one edge she could have sworn had always been there was, now, simply not.

She decided she'd misremembered. She had, after all, misremembered a great deal in three years of grief. Telepathy, when she mentioned it in passing, agreed that memory played exactly these kinds of tricks, and neither of them thought about it again for some time.

### The name that came later

Late in their search — after records that led nowhere, after names that dissolved into aliases that dissolved into nothing — an informant they'd paid handsomely for very little useful information leaned in before taking his payment and said, almost as an afterthought, that people like them tended eventually to come to someone's attention. Not APSC's. Not the Council's, whatever that was. Someone else, he said, who kept things. Who kept people, sometimes, the way other collectors kept anything rare enough to be worth the trouble.

He wouldn't give a name. He wasn't sure he had one to give.

Neither Reign nor Telepathy pursued it further that night. There was too much else in front of them that had a name already, and a shape they understood, and an answer that felt closer than it had in years.
