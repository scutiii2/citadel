# The Wall That Wanted Feeding
## Part One The Founders

Before the Council existed, before the word variant meant anything to anyone, there was a man who called himself nothing and let others call him The Traveler.

He crossed time the way other people crossed rooms — stepping from one moment to the next without ceremony, without visible seams. When someone broke history, he went back and mended it. When a hopeful fool tried to save a drowned brother or unmarry a father, The Traveler arrived, watched long enough to understand the shape of the damage, and closed it.

For a long while, that was the whole of his purpose. He fixed what people broke. He asked for nothing in return, because there was no one who could give him anything he needed.

Then he found the seam that didn't close.

A leap into the past, if unsecured, didn't always rewrite the traveler's original history. Sometimes it split it instead — produced a new thread running parallel to the old one, both true, neither canceling the other. The Traveler called these threads time-strings. Most died quietly, merged back into some larger possibility, or unraveled once the person who'd caused them stopped existing. Some did not. Some grew teeth.

He wrote the first law of what he was already, privately, calling Multiversal Peace a timeline may bend, but it must not be wrenched into contradiction again and again by the same stubborn hand.

During those early years he enforced the law personally, and without patience. He did not imprison the men and women who broke it. He ended them, cleanly, before their damage could compound. It worked. It also cost him something he never spoke about afterward, and eventually he went looking for another way to be right without being merciless.

He found it by accident, on a crossing that took him nowhere he intended.

### The dimension with no clock

Outside the ordinary run of space and time, he found a pocket sealed behind something like a membrane — thin, invisible, and absolute. Step through it and the clock simply stopped noticing you. A person could live inside that stillness without aging a day. They could watch a hundred separate moments of the outside world at once, the way a man on a hill watches several roads. And, crucially, once inside, they could no longer reach back out and touch the history they'd left.

A dangerous traveler, The Traveler understood, did not have to die. They could be lifted out of their own timeline and set down here, in the windless room behind the world, where their hands could no longer do harm.

He gathered six others he trusted with the same weight he trusted himself, and the seven of them built something none of them had a template for a government behind time, drawing its authority from the simple, defensible claim that removing a threat did not require destroying it.

They named it the Council Out-of-Time.

For a while it worked exactly as promised. A person who agreed to stop interfering with their own history could live an ordinary, unhurried life inside the dimension — could work, marry, raise children who would never age past a certain year. Only those who refused, who kept reaching back into the world to bend it toward themselves, were removed for good. It was more mercy than the world outside had ever offered dangerous men, and for two generations, the Founders believed they had solved something.

They had only postponed it.

### What a sanctuary costs

The dimension was vast. It was not endless. The membrane holding it apart from the rest of existence could only carry so much living weight before it began, in small and then larger ways, to strain.

The Founders discovered this the way most governments discover their limits — too late to choose gently. Every soul they'd saved was, in some measurable sense, a cost against the walls that kept them safe. Let the population climb forever and the walls would eventually give, throwing every resident back into ordinary time at once, in whatever century and body they happened to occupy at the moment of collapse.

They tried the decent solutions first. They slowed admissions. They searched for a way to reinforce the membrane itself. None of it was enough, and eventually the seven of them sat with an arithmetic that had no comfortable answer let the walls fail, or decide who got to stay behind them.

They chose the second option and told themselves it was temporary.

It began reasonably. The dimension needed engineers, so engineers were kept. It needed physicians, so physicians were kept. Experienced travelers were kept because someone had to police the timelines that still needed policing. Then the standard slid, the way standards do when the people setting them are frightened and tired the merely useful were kept over the merely present, the compliant kept over the difficult, and eventually — so gradually that none of the Founders could later say exactly when it happened — people who simply disagreed with them began appearing on the same lists as people who were dangerous.

No one had meant to build a machine for discarding the inconvenient. They had built one anyway.

### The missions nobody was meant to survive

It was a quiet administrator, not one of the Founders, who noticed that the problem of overcrowding and the problem of external threats could be solved with the same stroke of a pen. The Council needed operatives willing to face temporal hazards too dangerous for ordinary personnel. It also needed fewer people breathing behind the membrane. Sending the second group to do the first group's job solved both, so long as no one asked too closely who was being selected, or why the survival rate on certain assignments never seemed to improve.

For a time, no one did ask. Then someone's brother didn't come back from a mission that should not have killed a man of his experience, and someone's daughter noticed the same pattern in a different unit, and the two of them began comparing notes with people who had lost people the same way.

They called themselves Truth Unmasked.

They wanted transparency at first — records made public, selection criteria explained, the population math shown openly instead of managed in the dark. Seventeen of the earliest members gathered outside the Founders' headquarters to say so, unarmed, expecting at worst to be ignored.

The Traveler came out to answer them himself. He told them, not unkindly, that the missions were dangerous and that some losses were unavoidable in dangerous work. They told him that unavoidable losses didn't cluster this precisely around the people the Council found most troublesome. The exchange did not stay calm. He gave an order he would spend the rest of his exceedingly long life declining to examine too closely, and when it was finished, seventeen people who had come to ask a question lay dead in front of the building meant to safeguard peace across every possible history.

The Council called it the suppression of an armed threat. Enough members had watched it happen with their own eyes that the story did not survive contact with them. What had been a movement asking for answers became, in the space of that afternoon, a war looking for a reason it no longer needed.

### The thing that happens when a person meets themselves

While the rebellion gathered its strength, an entirely separate problem announced itself, and its timing could not have been worse.

A newly arrived resident fell ill within hours of entering the dimension — headache, nausea, nothing that pointed anywhere in particular. Elsewhere, unconnected to him by any record the Council kept, another resident began suffering identically. Then both of them started remembering things that had never happened to them childhoods, marriages, deathbed conversations, entire unlived lives arriving unasked, the way a stranger's coat might be handed to you by mistake.

They were the same person. Two branches of one causal identity, kept properly apart by an ordinary timeline's structure, thrown together by a dimension that recognized no such separation.

When they finally found each other and touched, both of them simply stopped being there. No injury. No struggle. Nothing left to bury.

The Council named the phenomenon a Blip and spent considerable effort determining its mechanism before finally understanding what its own sanctuary had done by pulling residents outside the flow that normally kept variant identities from ever occupying the same room, it had created the exact collision ordinary history prevented by design. Two versions of one life, brought into proximity, could not both continue.

The first policy was almost gentle. The earlier arrival became the Verified Variant, the later one a Duplicate, and the two were simply to be kept apart — no execution required, only distance. But a Council freshly humiliated by seventeen dead protesters and a rebellion it could feel gathering in its own hallways had lost its taste for gentle solutions. Keeping two people successfully separated forever was hard. Removing one of them was not. The policy tightened until it covered everyone, guilty or blameless, powerful or not, and Truth Unmasked took the new rule as final proof that reform had never been anything but paperwork.

### The night the Founders died

The rebellion did not stay disciplined. It rarely does. Loyalists fought in corridors where children had been born and raised without ever seeing an unaltered sky, and Truth Unmasked itself split over how far the reckoning should go — trial, or simply blood. Blood won the argument the way it usually does, by being faster.

Six of the seven Founders died that night, in the building they had built to be the safest place in any version of history.

The seventh appeared to die with them.

He had not. For years, quietly, alongside every other duty of running an impossible government, The Traveler had studied the one boundary his ordinary abilities could not touch from the inside — the membrane itself — and on the night his creation finally turned on him, he found the seam he'd been hunting and stepped through it into whatever lay beyond. He left behind a body burned past any test that existed to identify it, positioned exactly where a search would expect to find him.

The rebels found seven corpses in the Founders' office and believed, entirely reasonably, that they had ended something. They declared victory. Their commander took the seat the Founders had occupied and called it the start of something better.

### Better, briefly

For a while it was. The new government dismantled the disposable-mission system, opened the records on the Massacre, executed reforms the old Council would have called reckless. The dimension, for the first time in a long while, held something close to the peace it had been named for.

But the new commander had inherited a wounded society, and wounded societies fear repetition more than almost anything else. He worried about infiltration. He worried about old loyalties surviving under new faces. As the elders of Truth Unmasked died off and their successors inherited only the fear of collapse without the memory of what had caused it, the commander's answer to that fear became control — loyalty checks first, then evaluations, then education that stopped pretending to be optional, then, eventually, memories quietly adjusted to keep obedience where persuasion had failed.

He exempted only himself from the process, on the theory that someone needed to remain capable of questioning the whole machine. He never seemed to notice that this was the exact shape of the thing his revolution had been built to end.

Variant policy hardened alongside everything else. Every new arrival was measured now — ability against ability, stability against stability, usefulness weighed on a scale that made the decision feel administrative rather than moral. Whichever version scored higher became the Verified Variant. The other was simply removed. No one had to decide, anymore, which life mattered more. They only had to decide which one was more convenient to keep.

### The Cleanser

The Traveler came back years later, under a different name and a face old enough that no one thought to look twice at it. He called himself The Cleanser, and his gifts made him instantly, uncomfortably valuable — the kind of operative sent after threats too dangerous to risk on anyone replaceable.

It was the sitting commander, not any suspicious analyst, who eventually worked out who he really was. Rather than expose him, the commander made him an offer that suited them both silence for service, protection for obedience. Only the two of them knew that one of the seven founding dead was still walking the halls he had built.

The Cleanser rarely failed. The Council came, over time, to treat him less like a person than like a tool too useful to question, which was precisely how he preferred to be treated — until the day the Council found a threat that made his silence impossible to maintain.

### Twenty-seven

A variant surfaced in the records under the designation Traveler-27BV, carrying the same fundamental gift as the man who had founded the Council that now hunted him — except this version used it exactly the way the first law had warned against, bending his own past again and again for reasons that were entirely his own. Every unsecured leap he made spawned another time-string. The count of affected histories climbed fast enough to frighten people who did not frighten easily.

The Council answered with its harshest instrument a Full Elimination Protocol, authorizing the destruction of every known variant carrying the Traveler identity, regardless of danger, regardless of guilt. Ascended or not. Powerful or not. It made no distinction, because distinction was exactly what the Council no longer trusted itself to make correctly.

The Cleanser was not assigned to it. That, more than anything, was what people noticed. He was the Council's strongest weapon and its most obvious choice for exactly this kind of threat, and his conspicuous absence did what absences always do in institutions built on secrets — it started people talking. The theory that formed was, this once, entirely correct The Cleanser was Traveler-Zero. Traveler-27BV was another version of the same man. The Council had built a machine to hunt its own founder without ever knowing it.

The Cleanser understood the Full Elimination Protocol would find him eventually if he did nothing. So he went looking for his other self first.

### What a Blip can become, if no one lets it be simple

When they found each other, neither one attacked. Both understood, better than any Council analyst, exactly what would happen if they touched — the same clean erasure that had claimed two strangers years before.

The Cleanser had spent centuries, in whatever way centuries meant to a man outside time, studying that erasure more closely than the Council ever had, and he had found something they'd missed. A Blip was not, strictly, death. It was a collapse of causal identity — two incompatible claims to the same life canceling each other out. But collapse assumed incompatibility. If two variants could somehow agree, before contact, to accept rather than contest each other's history, the theory held that convergence might replace erasure.

No one had tried it. He decided that not trying was no longer an option available to him.

He reached out and touched his other self, and the two lives did not cancel. They merged — every memory, every choice, every death and survival folding into a single continuous mind that had, until that moment, believed itself to be two separate men making two separate mistakes.

What walked away from that meeting was not The Cleanser, and it was not Traveler-27BV. It called itself nothing for a long while, the way its earliest self once had. The Council, once it understood what had returned, gave it a name of their own.

### Havoc

He came back to the dimension he had built knowing every weakness in it, because he had designed most of them himself, in gentler decades, for reasons that had made perfect sense at the time. The Council that had hunted its own founder without recognizing him had no answer for a man who knew its architecture better than its own engineers.

He tore it down. Its leaders died with it. Its records burned. Whatever authority the Council had claimed over any timeline ended in a single night that history would eventually disagree about entirely, depending on which version of it survived to be told.

The damage did not stop at the institution. In destroying it, Havoc tore the membrane itself — the ancient, unexplained boundary that had kept the dimension outside time in the first place. Ordinary time began leaking through the wound, and for a while nobody understood what was happening to them until they noticed they were aging again, that yesterday had finally, irreversibly, become different from today.

A pocket dimension that had spent generations standing apart from causality now had causality inside it, and causality, once admitted, does what it always does. It branches. The first fracture became a dimension-string of its own — an entire reality, splitting away the way an ordinary time-string once split from an ordinary timeline. Then another fracture. Then more than anyone still living could count.

In one string, the Council never fell. In another, Truth Unmasked never rebelled at all. In another, the New Council never learned to fear its own reflection long enough to become it. Every string, given enough time, grew its own version of a Council, because the same impossible arithmetic — too many people, too fragile a wall — waited at the end of every one of them.

And every Council, in every string, eventually found the records of the man who had built the first one and destroyed the second, and gave him, independently, in language after language, the same name.

He kept moving between the strings after that, and the pattern of what he chose to destroy told its own story, if anyone had been positioned to read it. He did not spare the Councils that had learned nothing. He did not spare the ones that hadn't yet had time to fail. He had reached a conclusion no one had asked him to share that the shape of the institution, not the character of whoever ran it, was the thing that always ended the same way. Fear built control. Control built resistance. Resistance built more fear. Somewhere down that road, no matter who started walking it with the best intentions in any version of history, another Council became indistinguishable from the one before it.

He meant to end the pattern. Every ending he made only produced more branches to repeat it in.
