# The Story He Did Not Know Was His

In a town whose name would not survive the century, a man with a worn instrument on his back told a story about a king who had given up his throne and lost his children to the sky.

He told it the way he told everything — lightly, for coin and applause, embroidering the sad parts until they were easier to laugh at than to feel. In his version, the king was a fool who mistook mercy for weakness. The crowd loved that king. Fools who lose everything make for better company than kings who lose everything for a reason, and the man had learned, over more years than he could number, which version an audience would pay for.

"And so," he said, raising his cup toward the low tavern rafters, "the fool sent his own children into the sky rather than admit he couldn't protect them on the ground. Two burned before they cleared the roof. One vanished. Two more built whole worlds without him and never learned his name."

The tavern laughed at the right places. Someone near the fire called out to ask whether the fool ever found the missing one.

"No one knows," the man said, and meant it more literally than his audience understood. "That's why it's a good story. Good stories don't finish. They just stop being told for a while."

He drank. The room moved on to wanting a different tale, one with a monster in it, and he gave them that instead, and somewhere behind his eyes, for the length of perhaps two heartbeats, something had tried very hard to surface and failed.

He did not notice the failure. He had stopped noticing most of them by then.

---

He had not always told stories for a living. There had been a time — long enough ago that even his memory of having forgotten it had mostly worn smooth — when he had believed knowing what would happen gave him the right to change it.

He had warned kings away from wars they were fated to lose, and watched some of them lose anyway, on schedule, as if his warning had only rearranged which soldiers died and not whether they would. He had loved a woman once, fully, the way only something that had never needed to love before is capable of loving the first time it tries, and time had taken her from him regardless of what he knew or didn't know about the shape of the future. He had built things meant to outlast grief and discovered that grief outlasts almost everything.

After that, he stopped intervening.

It was not surrender, exactly, though it looked enough like surrender that he stopped correcting people who called it that. It was closer to the decision a man makes after enough failed surgeries — that his hands, however capable, had done more damage reaching in than they had done good.

So when he saw a king marching toward a battle that would end him, he no longer warned the king. He told a story about a king who marched toward a battle that ended him, and let the actual king overhear it if fate arranged the seating that way, and did not follow up to see whether the overhearing had changed anything.

When he saw a woman falling in love with a man who would eventually ruin her, he did not step between them. He told a story about a woman and a man like that, in a town two rivers away, where no one present had any stake in the outcome, and moved on before the story could grow roots.

It was easier that way. Easier for him, at least. Whether it was easier for the people whose futures he declined to touch was a question he had stopped being equipped to answer honestly, because by then he could no longer always tell, from the inside, which of his stories were warnings and which were simply stories.

---

Centuries do not pass evenly for a man who does not age through them. They accumulate instead, the way silt accumulates at a river's mouth, indistinguishable grain from indistinguishable grain, until one day the man realizes the riverbed itself has moved and he can no longer say when.

He watched kingdoms fold into ruin and ruin fold into hillside and hillside erode into something no one would ever again recognize as having once held a throne room. He watched oceans migrate. He watched a desert bloom into forest and, several lifetimes later, watched the same forest burn back down into the same desert, as if the land itself had only been trying out two possible futures and hadn't yet decided which one it preferred.

His memory did not keep pace with any of it. Faces went first — he would meet a woman and feel certain, with no evidence to support the certainty, that he had loved someone with her exact laugh several ages ago, and never be able to recall whether that was true or simply the shape loneliness takes after enough centuries of it. Names went next, then the places those names had belonged to, then — this was the loss that frightened him most, in the years he could still be frightened by anything — the specific reasons he had once had for setting out on any of this in the first place.

He tried, for a while, to hold the losses off with method. He kept notebooks of names. He drew maps of worlds he was afraid he'd stop being able to place. He wrote, longhand, across seasons, an account of his own life, meaning to anchor himself to it the way a ship anchors to a seabed it can no longer see.

He finished the account on a winter evening in a room he would not remember three years later, and made the mistake of reading the first page back to himself.

He did not recognize the man it described.

He burned it before he could decide whether that was mercy or cowardice, and afterward found that he felt something close to relief, which frightened him more than the burning had.

From then on, he stopped trying to hold on to himself. He turned his attention outward instead, toward everyone else's stories, because those, unlike his own, did not require him to remember anything he had lost the right to claim.

---

There were more stories than any single mind should have been able to hold, and he held them anyway, because holding them had become the only shape his existence still had.

He watched a woman die in one place while, in some other place he could not have named the distance to, a child took her first breath at the exact same hour, as if the universe had simply moved one soul's ledger entry to a different column rather than closing the account. He watched a city fall to fire while, elsewhere, an identical city's foundations were being laid by masons who would never know their work echoed a ruin they'd never see. He watched a man face a choice and — in some fold of existence he had no ordinary way of reaching, and no ordinary way of explaining afterward — watched the same man, wearing a different fate, choose differently.

He came, slowly and without any single moment he could point to as the turn, to understand something that should have terrified him and instead simply exhausted him into a kind of peace: time was not a road he was walking along. It was not behind him, or ahead of him. It did not have a direction that applied to what he had become.

Every story he told was true somewhere, because somewhere was no longer a place with borders. A king who had never existed in the town he was drinking in had absolutely existed in some other fold of what could be. A war he invented on the spot to entertain a room full of strangers had been fought, in full, complete with its grief and its survivors, in some other accounting of events he would never personally visit. He was not lying when he told his stories. He had simply stopped being able to promise which version of true he was describing on any given night.

That was the part no listener ever understood, and the part he stopped trying to explain to them. To the people drinking in front of him, a story was a thing that had happened, once, in a place with a name, or else it hadn't happened at all and calling it true was simply a kindness extended to a good performance. To him, by then, a story was closer to a room in a house too large to ever finish walking through — real the moment you stood in it, real for as long as anyone stood there with you, and no less real for the fact that most people would only ever see one room and assume the house ended at its walls.

---

Different places gave him different names, over the ages, none of which he corrected and none of which he claimed. The Dreamer. The Wonderer. The Man Without a Name, which came closest to accurate and pleased him least, because it implied a name had once existed to be without.

He answered to whichever one a given fire's worth of strangers offered him, the way a man answers to whatever a river happens to be called on either side of a border he's forgotten the name of himself.

One name outlasted the others, though he could never afterward say which town, or which century, had first offered it to him. It began, as far as anyone could reconstruct, as a joke — a farmer, annoyed at being outwitted in an argument about whether a story he'd just been told could possibly be true, throwing up his hands and declaring that arguing with the man was pointless, since apparently he already knew everything worth knowing, better than anyone else could hope to.

The name stuck the way jokes sometimes do, long after the joke itself has been forgotten by everyone who first laughed at it.

He did not choose it. He simply stopped correcting it, the way he had stopped correcting all the others, and somewhere in the slow accumulation of centuries during which he answered to it without ever quite deciding to, it became less a name given to him and more a description of what was left once nearly everything else about him had worn away.

Not because he remembered everything.

Because he had forgotten enough of himself that there was finally room for everything else.

---

He no longer traveled toward anywhere. He traveled because a fire was always burning somewhere ahead of him, and a fire meant people, and people meant a story waiting to be asked for.

On the night this account finds him last, he sat at the edge of one such fire, his instrument silent across his back, listening to a child ask whether the tale he'd told an hour earlier — about a fool king who had sent his own children into the sky rather than watch strangers take them — had really happened, or whether he had made it up the way grown-ups made up most things when they wanted a child to fall asleep faster.

He looked at the child for a moment longer than the question required.

"Somewhere," he said, "it happened exactly the way I told it. Somewhere else, it happened a little differently. And somewhere, probably, it's still happening right now, to a king who hasn't lost his children yet and doesn't know tonight is the night he will."

The child considered this with the total seriousness only children and the very old are capable of, and asked which version was the true one.

He did not answer immediately. Whatever had almost surfaced in that first tavern, in that first telling of the same story, stirred again, closer to the surface than it had come in longer than he could measure — a window. A crowd he couldn't quite picture. A weight in his chest shaped exactly like five names he could no longer read even though some part of him was certain, in that instant, that he had once known every one of them by heart.

It passed, the way it always passed.

"All of them," he told the child instead, and meant it, because by then it was the only honest answer left available to him. "That's what makes it worth telling twice."

He added a log to the fire. Somewhere beyond the reach of that light — beyond the fire, beyond the town, beyond the particular fold of existence in which an old man with no name he could fully claim sat telling stories to a child who would grow old and forget most of them — the story continued anyway, in every place it had ever been or would ever be true, waiting, patiently, the way it always had, for someone to sit down and tell it again.
