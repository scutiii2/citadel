# The Second Time the Sky Opened

A woman named Corvessa Thane spent the last conscious minute of her old life watching a planet she had never seen before rush up to meet her, and thinking, with the strange calm that sometimes arrives exactly when calm is least useful, that at least the fall was pretty.

She had been a magistrate's clerk. Before that, unimportant. The pod had no window worth the name, only a scarred porthole the size of a hand, but it was enough to show her green where she had expected only rock, and blue where the manifest had promised nothing but a penal outpost too far from anywhere to matter.

She did not know the planet already had a name among the people who lived on it. She did not know it had three names, in three different mouths, none of which agreed. She struck the ground still not knowing any of that, and woke two days later in a room built by hands twice the length of her own, tended by a face that was almost human and was not, and the first thought she managed to form clearly was that whatever this was, it had chosen, for reasons she could not begin to guess, to save her instead of letting her die.

She would spend the rest of her life trying to earn that choice back. Her grandchildren would spend theirs forgetting it had ever needed earning.

---

To understand why that face bent over her without hesitation, it is necessary to go back much further than any prison ship — back to a night, if nights meant anything yet, when two vessels the length of small moons struck a planet hard enough to remake it.

The Ascendants inside those vessels did not die on impact. Their bodies remained where the wreckage held them, but what they were stopped, gradually and then entirely, being separable from the world around them. Their power moved into stone, into water, into the marrow of every living thing that would ever grow there, and settled, and slept, and kept working long after the sleeping began.

From that unconscious labor came the Deities — tall, unhurried, built to a scale that made the world's later inhabitants look, by comparison, like something added as an afterthought. They did not know what had made them. The two sleeping shapes beneath the ground became, over the ages, first a memory, then a mystery, then simply a story told to children about why the earth in certain places still felt warm to a bare foot at midnight.

The Deities built cities meant to outlast weather. Some of what they raised is still standing, mistaken by later eyes for the work of a much smaller and much more recent species. They lived, for a very long stretch of their history, without needing to share the planet with anyone.

Then the first humans arrived — not conquerors, not even settlers by choice. Cargo. A civilization far away had decided this world was distant enough to be useful only as a place to put people it no longer wanted, and so it sent them, the same way it would go on sending them for millennia afterward, without much curiosity about what they'd find when they landed.

What they found terrified them. What found them chose, for reasons the Deities themselves might not have fully agreed on among each other, to help rather than to hunt.

It is worth pausing on that choice, because everything that came after depended on it, and because it was not unanimous. Among the Deities who gathered around that first stranded settlement, there was an elder — her name has not survived, though her decision has — who argued that creatures so easily broken by their own new home would only ever be a danger to it eventually, and that the mercy about to be extended would not be remembered as mercy for very long.

She was outvoted. She was, in the way of these things, also correct, though it would take her people several hundred years to find that out.

---

Kindness, given time, becomes proximity. Proximity, given more time, becomes something neither side had a word ready for. Deities and humans built lives beside each other, and some of those lives became one life, and the children of those unions looked human enough to walk unremarked through either world, while carrying, folded somewhere beneath the skin, an inheritance neither world could fully claim.

The Hybrids did not all grow up the same. Those raised inside Deity settlements bent, generation over generation, toward what their Deity parent had been — until their descendants stopped being called Hybrids at all, and became instead a people of their own: the Enchants, bonded to the living current of the world the way their ancestors had been born already bonded to it. Those raised inside human settlements kept more of their human shape and less of their Deity strength, and their descendants, generation over generation, became the Evolved — stronger than ordinary humans, sharper, harder to kill, but never quite believing, not even among themselves, that the difference made them something other than human.

Two branches from one root. Neither more legitimate than the other. Both, for a while, at peace with what they were.

The peace did not survive contact with fear.

---

It began the way these things generally do — small, deniable, and completely irreversible. A human settlement, remembering enough of the civilizations it had come from to distrust anything larger and stranger than itself, attacked a Deity town near its border. The Deities defended themselves and won every engagement individually and lost the war anyway, because humans, whatever they lacked in strength, arrived in numbers no single defense could outlast.

What followed did not stay contained to one settlement, or one grievance, or even one generation. Deity cities emptied into forests and underground shelters. Hybrids were forced to choose a side neither side fully trusted them to be loyal to, and chose, in the end, every possible way a person can choose — some for the Deities who had raised them, some for the humans who shared their blood, some simply for whichever side offered to leave their own children alone.

Then the war stopped being local at all. The greater Intergalactic War, already grinding through a hundred other systems, finally reached the planet that had been trying, badly, to have its own smaller war in private. Whatever arrived from beyond Earth's sky did not distinguish between the combatants already fighting there. Human settlements burned beside Deity cities. Evolved communities vanished beside Enchant ones. When it finally stopped — not because anyone had won, but because there was, at last, almost no one left with the numbers to keep fighting — four peoples stood in the wreckage of what had once been four separate claims to the same world, and none of them had enough left to make a fifth claim worth pursuing.

Peace arrived the way it does after exhaustion rather than forgiveness. It is a colder kind of peace, and it lasts exactly as long as no one is forced to remember what caused the war in the first place.

For a long while, no one was.

The Deities rebuilt in what territory remained theirs. The Enchants gathered into small communities near the surviving Deities, close enough to keep the old alliance breathing, distant enough from the human remnants to keep the old wound from reopening by accident. The Evolved rebuilt alongside what was left of humanity, because that had always been the branch closer to human shape and human memory, and it stayed that way now by simple proximity.

Generations passed. The war became history. History, patiently, became folklore, and folklore does not insist on accuracy — it only insists on being retold. Deities and Enchants kept the truth close, inherited directly from grandparents who had lived it. The Evolved, embedded more deeply in human settlements than in their own separate memory, inherited a softer, more forgivable version, in which the fault sat more evenly, or not at all, on anyone still living.

Then the sky opened again, and a magistrate's clerk named Corvessa Thane fell out of it, knowing none of this, into a world that remembered everything she didn't.

---

The three surviving peoples had every reason, this second time, to let the newcomers die the way the first Deity elder had once predicted they eventually would.

They did not.

The Deities offered food and shelter to strangers who could not have named a single Deity city if their lives depended on it, which, in the earliest weeks, they very often did. The Enchants healed injuries the newcomers didn't understand had been healed, using methods the newcomers assumed were simply medicine. The Evolved — closest in shape, closest in memory, closest in blood to the people falling out of the sky — taught them how to eat, build, and survive a world that still killed the unprepared efficiently and without malice.

The newcomers never learned there were three separate peoples helping them. The Deities were unmistakably other, too large and too strange to be mistaken for anything human. But the Evolved and the Enchants looked, to eyes that had no reason yet to look closer, exactly like people — a little stronger here, a little stranger there, put down to family trait rather than to four separate branches of one very old event. The Evolved, for their part, rarely corrected the assumption. Neither did the Enchants, at first, though for entirely different reasons — the Evolved because the mistake cost them nothing and bought them belonging, the Enchants because the truth had never once, in their people's memory, bought anyone anything but grief.

Corvessa Thane spent forty years believing the woman who'd nursed her back from the crash was simply a neighbor with an unusual gift, and died without ever learning otherwise, and was, in this, entirely representative of her generation.

---

It could have stayed that way. For a while, remarkably, it did — an unlikely, unearned stretch of decades in which four peoples who each remembered a different amount of the same war lived close enough to trade, to marry, to raise children who knew nothing of any of it.

But settlements grow the way settlements always grow, which is to say past whatever border first contained them, and memory fades the way memory always fades in the people who were never taught to carry it — faster in the ones who have the luxury of forgetting, slower in the ones who do not.

The Enchants remembered. That was the trouble, in the end — not villainy on any side, not a single clean cause a historian could point to and call the reason, only the oldest and least original problem there is: some of the people living beside each other could forget the last war, and some of them could not, and no war has ever been settled purely by asking the second group to try harder.

An Enchant elder named Ysbel Corr — one of the very last living people who had heard the old war described by someone who had actually fought in it — stood before her own community's council when the first serious border dispute reached them, and said only this:

"They do not know what their grandfathers did to ours."

A younger voice from the same council answered her: "Then tell them."

Ysbel did not answer that directly. She looked instead at the border map spread across the table between them, at the human settlement markers creeping, year over year, closer to lines that had once meant something and now meant nothing to anyone who hadn't drawn them.

"Telling them," she said eventually, "has never once stopped anyone from taking what they already believed was theirs to take."

It was not agreement. It was not refusal either. It was the answer of a woman who had watched one war begin from exactly this kind of small, reasonable-sounding dispute, and did not believe explaining the last one would prevent the next.

She was right not to believe it.

---

The dispute that finally broke the peace has been remembered at least four different ways by the four peoples who were there for it, and no version fully agrees with any other. Humans say the Enchants struck first, over territory that had never been properly settled between them. Enchants say the humans had been warned, repeatedly, and chose expansion over caution anyway. The Evolved, caught geographically and by blood between both accounts, tend to say only that it was fear, on every side, wearing whatever justification came to hand first.

What is agreed on is smaller and colder than any of the explanations: a human settlement moved against a small Enchant community near a border that had held, without incident, for three generations. The Enchants fought back with everything the old bond to the living world could give them. The humans answered with everything their numbers could give them. The Deities, watching a shape they recognized far too well beginning to repeat itself, moved to intervene, and the Evolved — closer to humanity in blood, closer to the Enchants in memory, belonging fully to neither side of the argument — found themselves forced, again, to choose.

This time, the humans doing the choosing did not even know what they were choosing between. They did not know the people they were fighting had once saved their grandparents' lives without asking for gratitude. They did not know the Evolved standing among them shared the same distant blood as the Enchants they were sending soldiers against. They did not know the Deities had extended mercy to their species twice now, unearned both times, and were watching a third betrayal assemble itself out of the same tired ingredients as the first.

They knew only that something in this world was resisting them, and that resistance, to a species with no memory of ever having been the fragile ones, looked exactly like provocation.

The old peace — cold, unforgiven, but real — did not survive the season.

Somewhere beneath all of it, two children of a being none of the four peoples remembered correctly went on sleeping, still unaware that the civilization built from their scattered power was, for the second time in its short remembered history, about to tear itself open along the same old seam.

And somewhere else on the same planet, closer to the surface than either sleeping sibling, a third child of that same being — arrived far later, mistaken by everyone who met her for something she was only a fragment of — had not yet learned that the war beginning around her had already happened once before, to people who no longer had any living memory of how it had ended, only of the fact that it had.
