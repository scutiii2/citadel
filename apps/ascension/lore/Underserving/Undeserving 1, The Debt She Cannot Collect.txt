# I. The Debt She Cannot Collect

The boy on the riverbank was seven, and he was drowning slower than boys usually drown, because the water there was cold enough to make his heart forget its hurry. His mother's voice came from very far away, from a place that seemed to be shrinking. Then a hand found his wrist, under the water, where no hand should have reached, and the cold stopped being the thing that was killing him and became, instead, something almost kind.

She did not look old or young. She never did. Her hair moved the way hair moves in water even when she stood on dry ground, and her eyes were the color eyes go right before they close. She held the boy's face between her palms the way a woman might hold a bowl she was deciding whether to keep, and for three full seconds, the length of time it takes a heart to remember it has work to do, she did nothing at all.

Then she pushed.

Not him. Herself. Something in her chest, some door she kept for exactly this purpose, and warmth went out of her hands and into his skull and down through his spine, and the boy's heart lurched back into its rhythm like a dropped stitch caught at the last possible pass. He coughed up half the river. He never remembered her.

She sat on the bank afterward with her knees drawn up, soaked to the collarbone, and watched his mother drag him into the reeds and cover his face in kisses and terror. She did not go to them. She never went to them. That part of the work was already finished, and the other part, the part that was hers alone, the part nobody thanked her for, was watching a life continue that she had, for three seconds, held the ending of in her two hands.

Men who study these things, in the ages when men still bothered studying them, called what she carried a gift, and disagreed for centuries about its name. Some said it was mercy shaped like a woman. Some said it was death that had grown tired of only taking and had learned, out of some private boredom, to give things back. Neither was right, and neither was entirely wrong, and she never corrected either of them, because there was no one to correct. She had been alone in the shape of what she was for longer than the river had been a river.

---

She was two things worn as one skin, and she had stopped trying to explain which one was speaking on any given day.

The first was the stillness that comes after, not before, never before, but after, when the pain has finished its business and there is nothing left to do but lie back and let the room go quiet. Some who met her in that mood called her Aya, though it was never her name; it was only the nearest word they had for the particular kindness of an ending that doesn't hurt anymore. She sat with the old in their final hour and hummed nothing in particular, and they went out of the world the way a candle goes out when someone finally, mercifully, closes the window.

The second was the pain itself, the part that comes before the stillness, undignified and endless and unwilling to be hurried. Some called this half of her Troy, and it was the half that stood in field hospitals and watched men take four hours to do what should have taken four minutes, the half that had to be present for every second of it because leaving early was not a thing she was permitted to do. She hated that half of herself the way a person can hate their own hand. She could not put it down. It was as much her as the other.

Between the two of them she had healed thousands and killed no one, because she had never once taken a life; she had only, on occasion, declined to argue with a life that had already decided to end, and stayed to make sure it did not have to end alone. That was the whole of her work. Watch. Ease, where easing was permitted. Take, where taking was the mercy. Walk on. Sometimes a body came to her already halfway gone, too weak to hold on by itself, too far past the edge for anyone else's hands to reach, and in those cases the mercy ran the other direction. She would find the door already cracked open and simply choose, instead of ending it, to close it again, and hand the rest of that life back to whoever was still inside it, no explanation offered, none ever asked for. She had never once seen the same face twice, because the same face never needed her twice, not in the way that mattered.

Except one.

---

Everyone meets death eventually. Most meet her once, and are finished with the meeting.

He met her more times than either of them ever counted.

The first time, he did not have a name yet, only a title, and by then he had already worn through two or three others he no longer used. She found him on a field so thick with the dead that the ground itself seemed to be breathing, one long shudder that never resolved into stillness, and she went to him the way she went to everyone, kneeling in the mud beside a body that should, by every rule the field obeyed, have already stopped being a body.

His heart had two beats between it and silence. She reached for the door the way she reached for it in everyone, and it was there, exactly where it should have been, fully formed and ready and correct.

It simply would not open the rest of the way.

She had never felt that before, not absence, not exemption, but refusal, a door built correctly and locked from a side she couldn't reach. She stayed with him anyway, past the point her work required, turning the strangeness of it over the way a person turns over a stone that shouldn't be warm.

Then his heart, on its own, without her help, decided to keep beating. He walked off that field alive, having met her fully and completely, having done everything a dying man does except finish.

She told herself this was simply an anomaly, one door among the uncountable doors she had opened that had, for reasons belonging only to it, refused to swing all the way. She did not go looking for him again.

She found him anyway. Years that did not touch his face touched hers only the way grief touches a face, thinning it, in tiny increments, from the inside, and across those years, on fields that had nothing to do with each other, under names that had nothing to do with each other, his death kept arriving on schedule, and kept, every single time, failing to finish. It was not devotion that kept bringing her to him. It was her work, unfinished, calling her back the way any unfinished work calls back the hands that started it. She had no more choice in returning to him than a tide has in returning to a particular shore.

He began, over that time, to read her returning as something else. As a woman coming back to a man she could not stay away from. It was, in its way, a reasonable mistake, from where he stood, someone kept arriving at his worst moments and staying longer than duty required, and there is only so much a lonely man can be expected to call that, besides love.

He asked her, once, in a room lit by a single lamp neither of them had bothered to move closer to, what she was looking for, out there on all her roads.

You, she said, which was true and not true in a way she did not try to untangle for him. Not you the way you mean it. The door in you that won't finish opening. I keep coming back because it keeps not closing.

Then stay until it does.

It might never.

Then stay anyway.

That was when she understood the shape of what he believed, and the shape of the correction she now owed him. He thought her returning was a choice she kept making about him. It had never been a choice. It was a task that would not release her, the way a splinter too deep to dig out does not release the hand that carries it, and mistaking one for the other was going to cost him more than her leaving ever would.

I don't love you the way you think I do, she told him, and there was nothing professional left in her voice by the time she finished the sentence. Everyone I've ever met, I finish with. You're the only door I've never gotten through. I'm not staying away from you because I don't care. I'm leaving because staying would mean admitting I've stopped trying to finish the work, and I don't know what's left of me if I do that.

He said he would wait, then, for however long it took the door to open on its own, and she told him, gently, because gentleness cost her nothing and refusal cost her everything, that he had misunderstood the offer from the very beginning. A man who cannot die is not a man death visits out of love. He is a debt she has never been permitted to collect, and a debt is not a home. If she stayed with the one thing she could never finish, she would stop being able to finish anything; the door in her own chest would find, at last, a room it preferred to every other room, and cease opening for strangers on riverbanks. The work would end. She could not let the work end for the sake of one unfinished door, however long she had already spent standing in front of it.

She left before dawn, the way she left everyone, except that with everyone else the leaving had been an ending she was permitted to witness to its close. With him it was only ever an interruption, the same interruption, over and over, across more of his life than either of them ever added up. She did not know, walking away from that lamp lit room, that she would think of it again in ages so distant the room itself would no longer exist in any language, nor that a version of him not yet born would carry the exact shape of her leaving forward into a hunger with no name attached, a thing reaching, again and again, for a door in its own chest, and finding, every time, that the door led nowhere.

She only knew, walking away, that she had done the one cruel thing her kindness had ever required of her, and that she would go on doing it, to him, every time his death called her back to a door that would not finish opening, for as long as his road refused to end.

---

She still walks. She has never stopped walking, and there is a rumor among the very old and the very frightened that if you sit long enough beside someone who is truly finished with living, she will come, and her hands will be warm, and it will not hurt the way you thought it would.

What none of them know, what she has never told anyone, because there has never been anyone she trusted with the shape of her own ending, is that she is looking for something on every road she takes, some door of her own that she has never once found unlocked. She gives rest to everyone who asks it of her. She has never once been given any in return. She does not call this grief, because grief is a thing that resolves, eventually, the way every death she has ever attended resolves, and this, whatever this is, does not resolve. It only walks beside her, patient as a debt, all the way to the next riverbank, and the next, and the one after that, forever, or for as close to forever as a spirit built for endings is permitted to come.
