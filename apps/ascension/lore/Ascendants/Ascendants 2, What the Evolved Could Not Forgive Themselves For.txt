# What the Evolved Could Not Forgive Themselves For

A boy named Ostrin could lift a transport crate that took four ordinary men to carry, and for the first nine years of his life, that had been enough.

He used it the way his father had used it, and his father's mother before that — to move stone, to run messages faster than any wheel, to catch a falling roof beam meant for someone else's child. The Evolved of his settlement lived exactly this way: strong enough to matter, never strong enough to rule. They built their cities with their own hands and called the arrangement fair, because for a long time no one had shown them anything to compare it to.

Then a delegation of Enchants passed through, on their way to somewhere more important, and one of them — bored, or careless, or simply young — called down a lightning storm to clear a flooded road in under a minute.

Ostrin watched four years of road work undone and redone in sixty seconds, and something in him that had been sleeping woke up ugly.

It was not envy, exactly. Envy implies wanting what belongs to someone else. What Ostrin felt, and what an entire species of Ostrins would go on feeling for generations, was closer to grief — the grief of discovering that your best had a ceiling, and other people's best did not.

The Enchants drew their power from the living world itself — forests, tides, the slow burn of a planet's deep core — and the strength of that bond determined how much they could hold. Given enough connection, they became guardians strong enough to defend the stars they loved, because a dying world made a diminished Enchant, and neither could really be separated from the other.

The Divine did not draw from any world at all. They served something above every world — a being their scripture named the Creator, though the name meant less to them as history and more as gravity, an obligation folded into the architecture of what they were. Heavenly Power moved through the willing the way blood moves through a body that has stopped questioning its own pulse. When the demand came, as it eventually came to every Divine Ascendant, to surrender that essence entirely, most gave it without argument. Those who refused were not punished so much as unmade — stripped of the Power that had defined them, cast down to a world built for exactly that purpose, and left there to learn what it felt like to be ordinary again.

Three kinds of power. Three separate answers to the same original question — how does a being reach beyond what it was born as. The Evolved were the only ones who had not yet found their own answer, and eventually the not-having became its own kind of hunger.

---

The first machines were almost innocent.

Armor, to make an Evolved body harder to break. Focusing rigs, to keep a burst of strength from tearing its own tendons apart. A man named Perc Dail built the first successful set in a barn outside a city that no longer has a name worth keeping, and when he lifted, in that armor, three times what his body alone could bear, the men who watched him wept the way people weep at something they had been told was permanently closed to them.

For a while it was enough to be encouraging.

Then encouraging stopped being enough, the way it always does.

Boosters replaced training. Artificial organs replaced the ones that failed under the new loads. The failures, when they came, came badly — bodies that overheated from the inside, energy that tore through muscle looking for an exit it hadn't been given, Ascendants who absorbed more than their frames could hold and simply stopped, mid-motion, as if a switch somewhere had been thrown. Perc Dail's own daughter died this way, in a lab her father had built, wearing an upgrade her father had approved.

He did not stop the program. He led the search for something better instead, and history has never fully decided whether that makes him a monster or simply a father who could not let his daughter's death be the last word on the subject.

What they found, eventually, was not on any world's surface.

---

It took eleven years of drilling beneath the crust of a planet called Merrow to reach it — a vein of ore older than the ambitions digging for it, humming faintly enough that the first survey team mistook it for equipment malfunction and nearly abandoned the site. Someone touched it anyway. Hexon, they named it, because names had to come from somewhere and nobody felt like being poetic six kilometers underground.

It did not simply hold energy the way a battery holds energy.

It answered power. It leaned into whatever Ascendant strength came near it and made that strength larger, indiscriminately, with no apparent interest in whether the body wielding it could survive the increase.

The first controlled tests killed everyone in the room. Raw Hexon burned through the men who carried it the way acid burns through paper — not an explosion, something worse, a kind of hunger that ate outward from the point of contact until there was no longer a point, or a contact, or very much left to call a person.

They kept going.

A woman named Sael Ambrus — Perc Dail's second engineer, and by most later accounts the only person on the project who ever fully understood what they were building — found the stabilization method eleven months after the first deaths, in a formula that owed as much to accident as to insight. What emerged from her work was quieter than raw Hexon, contained, almost gentle by comparison. Pure Hexon, they called it, and when an Evolved Ascendant took it into their body for the first time and did not die, and did not scream, and simply stood a little straighter than they had a moment before, the room went silent in a way that had nothing to do with grief.

They had found their answer.

Pure Hexon did not overwrite what an Ascendant already was. It reached toward the bond a person already carried with their source — muscle and instinct for an Evolved, root and current for an Enchant, the old unbroken thread to the Creator for a Divine — and made that bond easier to hold. Power that would once have overwhelmed a body became power that body could simply use. This was called Pure Ascension, and it did not end at a single threshold. The bond, once strengthened, could be strengthened again. And again. Some Ascendants who underwent it climbed a distance from where they started that would have looked, to their own younger selves, like a different species entirely.

Sael Ambrus noted, in a report almost no one read carefully enough at the time, that Pure Hexon did not always require touch. Twice, Pure Ascension had occurred without any Hexon present at all — once during an event that should not have been possible under any law the Ascendants understood, a dead man drawing one final breath four minutes after his heart had stopped for good, long enough to save the child he'd been carrying before he stopped again for the last time. Reality, in these rare cases, seemed to fracture just enough to let something through. They called these fractures Paradoxes, and no one ever succeeded in causing one on purpose, which did not stop several people from trying, sometimes at terrible cost to whoever happened to be standing nearby when a Paradox chose to occur.

Because that was the part the reports underplayed, the part Sael Ambrus herself only admitted late in her life, to a granddaughter who never repeated it publicly: Pure Ascension was never free. Something had to feed the strengthening bond, and if the environment couldn't supply enough on its own, the Ascendant's body reached past itself and took it from whatever was near — a field of crops turned to ash-grey stalks overnight, animals found dead with nothing visibly wrong with them, a village outside one testing ground that woke up one morning simply tired in a way that never fully lifted. Nobody proved a connection. Nobody had to. The people who lived near testing sites knew, the way people always know the thing no one in charge will confirm.

Sometimes the Ascendant's own body could not survive what it demanded of everything around it, and the Ascension took the body too.

That should have been the end of it.

It was not, always.

---

There is a difference, thin as a blade's edge, between an Ascendant whose body fails and an Ascendant whose body is used up entirely. In the second case, on rare and terrible occasions, the life essence still surging through the dying frame found nowhere else to go and forced its way back into the corpse it had just vacated.

The body rose again.

It was not the same body, in any sense that mattered. The bond that Pure Hexon strengthened, this new power corrupted instead — twisting toward pain as its fuel, rage as its accelerant, blood as the closest thing it had to nourishment. A Crimson Ascendant healed wounds that should have ended them by spending the blood already spilled, grew stronger with every act of violence committed, and grew hungrier the stronger they became, in a loop that had no natural floor and, so far as anyone had discovered, no ceiling either.

Crimson Hexon followed not long after — a substance born from the same unstable processes, carrying the same appetite in concentrated form, changing anyone who touched it more fully than it empowered them. There were things called Corruption Parasites, born from Hexon reactions gone wrong in ways too specific to be predicted twice, and these attached to hosts, or consumed them, or in the rarest and worst cases, grew alongside a host until neither one could be called separate from the corruption anymore.

And still separately from all of this — not corrupted, not stolen from the dead, simply unwilling to keep losing organs to a body that failed faster than it could be repaired — the Evolved kept replacing themselves piece by piece. An arm. A heart. A spine. Eventually entire nervous systems rebuilt in silicon and alloy, minds that could reach into machine networks the way an Enchant reached into soil, consciousness itself backed up somewhere that fire and blood couldn't reach. They called themselves Synthetic Ascendants, and they were, in the end, the most honest of everyone on this list — they had never pretended their strength came from anywhere holy. They had simply refused to accept the strength they were born with, and kept refusing, patiently, one replaced organ at a time, until refusal became its own form of power.

Three kinds, once.

Six, now.

Pure. Divine. Abyss. Enchant. Synthetic. Crimson — each one a different answer to the same original grief a boy named Ostrin had felt watching a storm clear a road that had cost him four years to build by hand.

The Evolved had wanted, more than anything else, to stop being the weakest voice in a conversation that never once asked their opinion. In trying, they had built miracles. They had also built monsters, and had rarely managed to keep the two apart for very long, and history — which is patient about these things in a way people rarely are — has still not finished deciding which of those outcomes it will remember first.

Somewhere among all of it, in records too scattered to have been gathered by any single mind yet, one question kept resurfacing in the margins of reports nobody in power wanted to prioritize.

Six paths had already been found by beings who refused to stay what they were born.

Nothing in any of those records explained why the number should stop there.
