# VII. What the Moon Kept, What the Dark Woke

The island had one working left in it, and it had never before been asked to spend the whole of itself on a single life.

What answered the king's plea was not a voice. It arrived the way the sea's judgment had always arrived, as a change in the quality of the air, and then as light, the full moon overhead pulling itself down through the smoke in a column that had no business being that steady in a wind still thick with ash. It found the girl first, because she was the nearest thing left on the island still holding the shape of a life worth returning, and it found the dog second, because he had not released her, not in dying and not after, and whatever judged such things on that island had apparently decided, in its last act, that a devotion held that tightly did not deserve to be pried apart even by death.

It was not the same kind of healing the Babaylans had once given the deserving with their hands. This was closer to the sea's own workings, a pouring rather than a mending, every scrap of power the island's slaughtered women had carried in their lives, every strand of whatever the Anito had channeled across all their long silent watching, gathered into a single current with nowhere else left to go and given, entirely, to a girl too young to understand what was being asked of her body.

It did not simply return her to the moment before the wound. It went further than that, because there was no smaller portion of itself left to spend. It reached into the dog's stilled chest at the same time it reached into hers, and rather than mend two separate lives it braided them, the way a single length of rope is stronger woven from two strands than either could be alone, until neither the girl nor the animal held a life anymore that was fully separable from the other's.

---

Something else rode that current into her, something the island had not intended to give and had no way, by then, of holding back. A king's grief, still fresh, still standing at the edge of that light with his hands wrapped around a body that was, in that same instant, ceasing to be a body he was allowed to keep holding. He had asked for her life. He had not asked to give anything of his own in exchange, but the working did not distinguish, in its last desperate spending, between what was offered and what simply happened to be closest, and a man's whole unfinished longing for a wife he had lost and never stopped reaching for poured into the same current as everything else, and settled into the girl the way water settles into whatever shape is offered it.

She opened her eyes a long moment later, and they were the eyes of a child who had been dead and was no longer, and somewhere behind that simple fact, unrecognized even by her, sat a woman's face she had never seen and would spend the rest of forever failing to place, an ache with no name attached and no memory to explain it, a door she would spend centuries trying to open without ever once understanding whose door it had originally been.

The dog rose the same moment she did, and did not leave her side again, not that night and not in any night that followed for as long as either of them could be said to exist. What the island had done to him could not properly be called a return. He had died protecting her, all of him spent in that single motion of staying, and what came back into his body carried that motion forward as its whole purpose rather than as one part of a longer life. He became, from that moment, the shape devotion takes when it has already finished dying once and has nothing left to lose by staying a second time.

---

The king did not walk away from that light. He was standing closest to it when it reached its final measure, and the island, having nothing softer left to offer a man who had done what he had done and asked what he had asked, took the only remaining thing it had use for, which was his body, and set it, unmoving, at the center of what had once been a ring of living Anito.

He did not become one of them exactly. The old stone figures had been carved by hands that loved what they were making. What the king became was carved by nothing at all, simply held, the way grief holds a person still long after the moment that caused it has passed, his face turned forever toward the place where a girl who was not his daughter had, for a handful of seconds, been dead in his arms. Travelers who found the island in later ages, the very few who ever did, would describe a stone figure among the older carvings that did not match the rest, its face too recognizably human, its posture too plainly kneeling, and would argue for generations about what it commemorated, never once landing on an answer that satisfied even the ones proposing it.

---

The island did not stop being what it had been, entirely. On the nights the moon came full, something in it still remembered mercy. The spirits of the Babaylans who had died defending it walked those nights the way they had always walked them, tending fires that no longer needed tending, singing to children who were no longer there to hear the songs, entirely unaware, in whatever peace they'd been left, that they had ever stopped living at all. They could not heal anyone anymore. Whatever gift had let their hands hover above the dying and choose life or release had gone into the girl along with everything else, and what remained of them on those bright nights was a kind of gentle, forgetful haunting, women continuing a life that had already ended, comforted by not remembering the ending.

On every other night, the island became something the mainland had no true word for.

The queen's death had left something behind on that shore that no one, not the eldest, not the king, not any Babaylan who had ever lived there, had known to watch for: a confusion and a hatred with nowhere honest to resolve itself, soaked into ground that had already, by the time of the invasion, absorbed two years of the king's own curdled grief and every dying breath the massacre produced. It had lain dormant through all of it, waiting the way certain kinds of rot wait, patient, unhurried, gathering rather than spending. The slaughter woke it fully. Every soul that had died on that island in the burning, Babaylan and soldier alike, rose again in the moon's absence not as themselves but as something caught at the exact threshold between finishing and not finishing, hungry for an ending none of them could reach, wearing what was left of their own faces the way a torn coat still holds the shape of the man who used to wear it.

They wanted only one thing, all of them, in every dark night that has followed since. They wanted the same rest the moon's mercy gave to the ones who died gently. It had been denied them, and a want denied long enough curdles the same way grief curdles in a king's chest, into something with teeth.

The whole of it, dormant and waking, wanting and hating, came in time to answer to a single name, given to it by the rare few who survived meeting it and lived long enough afterward to name anything: Twilight. She slept when the moon was full, folded back into whatever quiet the light still afforded her. She woke in every dark between, walking the ruins with all her drowned and burned and unfinished dead behind her, seeking whatever the moon had once given the island's true healers the power to give and finding, every single night, that she could take a great many things but never once the one thing she wanted for herself or for the countless hungry shapes that followed her.

---

There is a night, rarer than either the full moon's mercy or the ordinary dark's hunger, when the moon stands full in the sky and something passes in front of it anyway, blotting the light without asking the sky's permission, the way the king had once done to a locked door he had no right to open. On those nights, by every account that has survived long enough to be repeated, the mercy that should have held does not hold, not entirely, not for the length of the shadow's crossing, and whatever wakes in that gap wakes larger than it has ever woken before, as though the years of hunger denied on every ordinary dark night had simply been saved, without anyone's notice, for a night when the door might, for once, be forced rather than found.

No one who has witnessed the whole of what happens during that crossing has lived to describe its end.
