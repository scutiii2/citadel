## A House Built From Other People's Grief

Centuries ago, before he collected anything at all, a boy wanted to be loved, and a witch told him he never would be.

He did not remember her face anymore. He remembered the shape of the field where she'd found him, and the particular cold of that night, and the two things she'd said before she left him standing in the wet grass. That he could not die unless someone loved him truly. That no one ever would.

He had carried those two sentences for so long that he could no longer tell where the witch's voice ended and his own began. Perhaps she had cursed every stranger who looked at him to feel disgust. Perhaps she had simply told a frightened, unloved boy that he was unlovable, and time had done the rest, the way water finds the same crack in a stone for a thousand years until the stone believes the crack was always meant to be there.

He only knew what he remembered: that every face that ever looked at him too long eventually turned away.

He had tried, once, to prove it wrong. He had helped strangers rebuild homes that floods had taken. He had given his coat to men who spat at him for it after. He had loved carefully, in small doses, waiting to see how long it would take before the warmth curdled into something else. It never took long. A friend borrowed money and vanished. A woman he'd nursed through fever called him monstrous the moment she was well enough to see him clearly in daylight. Someone he trusted with the location of everyone he had ever protected sold that list for a price he never learned.

That last one had a name. He did not let himself think it often. Maze.

Eventually he stopped testing the curse and started managing it instead.

He learned that grief left things behind — a letter, a photograph, a coat still smelling faintly of someone who no longer wore it — and that he could not bear to let those things be thrown away, because if the objects vanished then the moments they belonged to would vanish too, and he would have nothing left to prove any of it had ever been real. So he began to keep them. A drawer became a chest. A chest became a room. A room became a house full of other people's discarded lives, and somewhere in that accumulation, a name attached itself to him without his choosing it. The Collector.

He did not choose the name any more than he had chosen the curse. He simply understood, eventually, that people needed to call a thing something, and this was the thing they had decided to call him.

### The Illusionist

He had known for a long time that he could make pictures out of nothing. He had never known what those pictures were for. Then one night, surrounded by everything he had kept, he tried to give one of them a shape that could move.

It moved.

He gave it a voice. It spoke. He gave it a purpose, and it walked to the door and opened it and closed it again, exactly as he'd asked, and stood there afterward waiting for the next instruction like a dog too well trained to need one.

He understood, watching it, that the objects were not merely proof. They were material. The more a thing had mattered to someone, the more convincing the shape he could build from it — a coat became a soldier's shoulders, a photograph became a face that could hold an expression, a wedding ring became a woman who would never again ask him for anything he couldn't give.

He called himself something else, for this. The Illusionist. Few people ever learned that the two names belonged to the same man. He preferred it that way. A collector gathers what is lost. An illusionist gives it somewhere to stand. Between the two of them, an empty house stopped being empty.

None of what he built could love him. He understood that going in. But none of it could leave, either, and for a man who had run out of ways to distinguish those two mercies from each other, that was enough for a while.

### Ghost

There had been an older name, from further back, that he wore again now the way a man returns to a coat he thought he'd outgrown.

He had built it long before any of this — in a life spent training a single unmarked recruit to survive a war fought entirely under false names, in a world where the wrong person learning your face could get everyone you loved killed. He had built codenames into that world like scaffolding, so that no name could ever again be used as a leash. He had trained that recruit past the point of mercy, and told himself it was necessary, and had never let himself sit too long with how much of it he had enjoyed — the one bond in his memory he could shape entirely by hand, that would never once decide, on its own, to leave.

He did not think of that often either. It sat beneath the rest of him the way a foundation sits beneath a house nobody remembers being built.

So when he needed, once more, to disappear completely — to help people from inside a darkness no one could see his face in — he reached for the same discipline without noticing he was reaching for anything old at all. The same silence. The same mask, cut from different cloth but built on the same understanding: that a face was a liability, and a name was a door someone could walk through uninvited.

In Xenus they began telling their own small story about him. That if something hunted you in the dark, you should run toward the darkest part of the street, not away from it. That something waited there that would not let you be taken.

No one learned his face. He killed only the ones he judged deserved it, and only then did he let them see him — his real face, the one the curse had ruined, the last thing many of them ever saw before the dark took them. He did not enjoy their fear. He understood fear from the inside too well to enjoy causing it in anyone but the guilty.

It did not occur to him, not once, that the same hands doing this quiet mercy work in the dark were about to do something far less merciful in the light.

### Telepathy

Then one of the most powerful sentients in the world died, and the shape of everything around that absence went unstable, the way water goes unstable around a hole punched through ice.

The Collector had, by chance, kept something that had belonged to the dead man. It was enough.

He built an illusion good enough to fool an entire company that its leader had never died at all. While that false face ran meetings and signed papers and reassured frightened people that nothing had changed, The Collector remained beneath it, unseen, doing the actual work of controlling everything the illusion touched.

A few people noticed the wrongness first. He removed them, one at a time, and kept something of each — and made new illusions from what he kept, so that the people who vanished never seemed to have vanished at all. Familiar faces kept returning to work. Familiar voices kept answering familiar questions. None of it was real. All of it was him.

He told himself, for a while, that this was simply the largest room he had ever built. A place where no one could leave, because none of them had ever truly been there to begin with. A world that could not reject him, because he had made sure nothing in it was capable of rejecting anything.

It was not the empty house anymore. It had people in it — real ones, underneath the illusions, still breathing, still afraid, still paying for a kindness he had once tried to offer the world and had long since stopped remembering how to offer honestly.

### The Mirror

There was one place he had always been careful with. The mirror dimension, and the things that lived inside it — not ordinary reflections, but sentients with a world of their own, patient in the particular way that only patient things can afford to be.

He had used them for years, the way a man uses any tool he has decided is beneath his conscience. When someone became a problem he could not otherwise solve, he brought them to a mirror in the dark of his own house, took something that belonged to them, and let the reflections do the rest. He kept the object. He made an illusion. To everyone else, the missing person had simply never left.

He did this for so long that he stopped counting the ones he'd given away.

The reflections did not stop counting.

For a long time they had no way out but the gateways he controlled. Then something changed — some threshold crossed inside their own strange dimension, some pressure built from all that he had fed them across all those years — and one night the wall of a room he thought was secure came apart, and there were not one or two of them standing in the wreckage, but many.

He ran. He set every alarm the building had. He sent every illusion he had ever built to hold the line, including the one still wearing a dead man's face, and watched them all get pulled through mirrors they could not break fast enough. He escaped while they fought a battle none of them could have understood they were fighting for him.

The reflections lost that skirmish, in the sense that their gateways were shattered to sand behind them. They did not lose the memory of who had made them what they'd become.

### The Man Who Hid

He disappeared again, the way he always eventually disappeared. No empire. No illusions walking around wearing borrowed faces. Just Ghost, and the dark, and the particular quiet of a man who has run out of new ways to be someone else.

He told himself it would be enough to wait them out. Things that lived outside of time, he reasoned, should eventually grow bored of things that lived inside it.

He was wrong about that too, though he would not learn how wrong for a long while yet — long enough to walk a hundred more roads, and to sit down beside a river one afternoon beside a girl who commanded water and a dog who did not trust him for exactly as long as it took to decide it didn't matter.

He did not know, sitting down beside that river, that something on the other side of every mirror he had ever broken was still patient. Still counting. Still his.

He only knew that for the first time in longer than he could measure, someone had looked at him and not looked away.
