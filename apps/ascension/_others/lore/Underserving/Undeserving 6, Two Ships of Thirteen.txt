# VI. Two Ships of Thirteen

The sea took eleven ships before dawn.

It did not take them the way it had once taken supplicants, gently, with tests a person could fail and walk away from. Weakened by the absent moon, the water still remembered its purpose, and what it lacked in judgment it made up for in blunt refusal, closing over hulls the way a fist closes over something it has decided not to release. Men who had rowed with grief in their arms for two years drowned within sight of the black sand they'd crossed an ocean to reach, and the king, on the deck of the twelfth ship, watched three of his captains go under without slowing his own oars, because stopping to grieve them was a luxury the crossing did not extend, not even to a king.

Two ships reached the sand. Bayani was on one of them, soaked to the collarbone, gripping a blade he had never used on anything larger than a fish, telling himself, in the last moments before the hull ground against the shore, that a locked door finally had a face on the other side of it, and that this, at last, would feel like an answer.

---

The Babaylans had known they were coming. Whatever the moon's darkness had cost the island's defenses, it had not cost them their knowing, and the eldest had spent the hours before the landing moving among the huts at the tree line with the same flat calm she had once used on a dying queen, telling the youngest of her sisters to take the children inland, telling the strongest to stand with her at the Anito, telling no one that she expected to survive the morning, because she did not, and there was no kindness in pretending otherwise to women who would need every clear thought they had left.

Sinag was among those who stood. Aurelio was not asked to, and stood anyway, a spear he barely knew how to hold shaking in hands that had spent the last six years learning to gentle sick children rather than harm grown men, because the alternative was letting Sinag face it without him, and some choices are not really choices at all once love has already made up its mind.

The fighting, when it came, did not resemble the stories that would eventually be told about it on the mainland, stories that would flatten two ships' worth of frightened, grieving men into a single righteous tide. It was smaller than that, and uglier. A soldier who had followed the king out of his own private grief, a brother lost to some ordinary illness the island had never even been asked to heal, found himself standing over an old woman with no weapon in her hands and could not make his arm finish the motion his orders required, and was cut down himself, by one of his own company, for the hesitation, a death that would never appear in any account the king later allowed to be written. Bayani killed a man near the Anito who might have been a Babaylan's husband or might have simply been another islander with no part in any judgment ever passed on anyone, and did not learn which, and would not, for the rest of his life, stop turning that not-knowing over in the dark the way his sister's death had once been turned over by strangers at her grave.

Aurelio died defending the door of the hut where Aya slept, a spear meant for someone else finding him instead because he had put himself in its path on purpose, and Sinag died within the hour beside the Anito she had tended her whole life, her hands, which had spent years hovering above the dying to test the heat of what they carried, closing for the last time around nothing at all.

Neither of them screamed her name as they went. There was no time left, by then, for anything but the going.

---

The eldest was the last of the Babaylans standing, and she stood a long time, longer than her body should have permitted, at the foot of the largest Anito, the one whose almost-face had watched every crossing the island had ever judged. She did not beg. She had spent a lifetime delivering verdicts without cruelty and received, in her final hour, no such mercy in return, and there was a kind of terrible symmetry in that which she was, in the very last moment, almost able to appreciate before the symmetry closed over her the way the sea had closed over the eleven ships.

The fires that followed burned for three days and three nights, huts and stores and the small boats the island had once used to ferry supplies from the mainland, and the smoke sat low over the black sand in a way that made the whole island smell, for a long time afterward, like something that had once been a hearth and had forgotten how to be anything gentler.

By the time the last fire burned down to something that could be walked through, the moon overhead had swelled back to full, fourteen days having passed the way fourteen days pass in war, unevenly, without anyone keeping careful count, and it hung over the ruined tree line with a light the island's own defenses would once have gathered and turned to strength, if there had been anyone left with the strength to gather it.

---

The king walked the ruin alone on that final night, his men either dead or too exhausted to accompany him, and found, at the edge of a collapsed hut near the largest of the broken statues, a sound he did not, at first, believe was real.

A small girl, perhaps six or seven, hidden in the space between two fallen support beams, was crying in the particular thin register of a child who has been crying for so long that her body no longer has the strength to make the sound properly. She was holding a dog against her chest, a squat, solemn animal gone entirely still, and she was drenched, the king saw as he came closer, in blood that was too much of it to belong to the dog alone.

He knelt in front of her the way he had once knelt in this same black sand eleven days into a different grief, and she looked at him with an expression that held no fear in it at all, only a kind of exhausted, bottomless confusion, as if the concept of danger had already used up every reaction she had left to give it.

She did not speak. Whatever she might have said, whatever name she might have given him for the man who had killed her mother and her father and every woman who had ever sung her to sleep at the evening fires, died in her throat before it reached her tongue, and a moment later, so did she, her small body going still against the dog she would not release even in dying, in the same held-breath quiet the king had watched his own wife go out with three years before.

He did not move for a long time. There was nothing left in him, by then, capable of the kind of grief that moves a body. There was only the recognition, arriving far too late to be useful to anyone, of the exact shape of what he had spent two years building, a shape that had a face now, small and slack and entirely undeserving of anything that had been done to reach it.

He thought of black sand under bare feet, years before, a dog too solemn for its size, a woman watching with a patience he had once envied. He understood, kneeling there with the last thing his war had made, precisely whose daughter he was holding, and precisely what that made him.

---

He did not know, kneeling in the ash with a dead girl in his arms and a full moon overhead lighting a ruin that had once been the only place on earth willing to heal what nothing else could, whether there was anything left on that island still capable of hearing a plea. He asked anyway, because asking was the only motion left available to a man who had run out of every other kind.

He did not ask for forgiveness. He understood, by then, that forgiveness was not his to request and would not have meant anything if it had been given. He asked instead, with his ruined hands wrapped around a girl who would never open her eyes again to hear him, for the one thing he still had the standing to ask for, the one debt he might, in some ledger far older than his own, still be permitted to settle.

He asked for her life back. Not his. Not his wife's, long past any asking. Only hers, and whatever it might cost him to have it returned.

The moon held steady overhead, full and indifferent, and for a long moment nothing in the ruined tree line answered him at all.
