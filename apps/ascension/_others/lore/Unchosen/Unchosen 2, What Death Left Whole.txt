## What Death Left Whole

There were two hearts inside her, and neither of them had asked to share a body.

One had belonged to a girl named Aya. The other had belonged to a dog named Troy. Death had tried, once, to take them separately. Whatever waited on the other side of it had not understood that the two of them had never learned how to say goodbye.

Aya woke with water moving around her fingers without disturbing them. Troy woke smaller than she remembered, rolled onto his back, and waited for her hand as though nothing at all had happened. She laughed before she cried, or cried before she laughed — she could never afterward say which came first.

"You're still here," she said.

His tail struck the ground. That was answer enough for both of them.

They learned the rules of it slowly, the way anyone learns rules that were never written down for them. Water came to Aya's hands the way breath comes to a chest — she could pull it from a river, gather it from the air on a dry day, or close a wound with it before the person bleeding understood they'd been touched. Troy could not be killed. He could be struck, buried, thrown, and drowned, and none of it held. Whenever a threat came near her, he was already moving before either of them decided he should.

A dog became a wolf when there was one enemy. A wolf became six when there were more than one. A wolf became something too large for its name when there was no other choice, and the ground remembered it afterward whether or not Aya asked it to be gentler.

Most of the time, when nothing needed killing, he was simply the dog again. He slept beside fires. He slept on boats. He slept through arguments Aya had with strangers about payment she never intended to accept. Soldiers who had forgotten how to rest sat down next to him and, without meaning to, remembered how.

There was a night, once, in a camp far from anywhere with a name, when a soldier was carried in already past saving — the kind of wound men did not survive, the kind everyone in earshot had already stopped hoping about. Aya knelt beside him anyway. She did not do anything anyone could describe afterward. She simply held his hand and waited, the way a person waits at a door they already expect to open the wrong way.

He lived. No one could say how. Aya did not explain it, and no one who saw her face in that moment thought to ask her to.

If Aya died, his soul stayed wherever she'd fallen until she came back to it. No distance changed that. No world between them changed it either. People called it a curse on both of them, or a blessing, or proof that one of them was the reason the other survived. None of them had the shape of it right. They simply did not know, between the two of them, how to be the ones left behind.

They crossed kingdoms that had stopped remembering their own names. They gave water to strangers in places that had none. They were feared in some towns and welcomed in others, sometimes on the same road, a day apart. People remembered the wolf. People remembered the bear that flattened men twice its size without malice, the way a landslide has no malice. But the people who knew her best remembered the dog who wandered into the middle of a battlefield, looked at everyone involved, and lay back down beside her as though none of it concerned him.

"Lazy," she told him once, scratching behind an ear he offered without lifting his head.

He closed his eyes. For a while, the world agreed to wait.

There was one absence Aya carried that never got smaller no matter how far they walked. A name she almost never spoke. Someone missing long enough that missing him had simply become one more thing she did, the way she breathed, or slept lightly, or disliked wasting food.

Among the people who came and went through the years that followed, there was a man who stayed longer than most. The first time he looked at her directly, she looked back a half-second longer than the moment asked for — the way a person checks a face against a memory they can't place, and doesn't find it, and lets the moment pass without remarking on it. He noticed. He did not know what he'd noticed. Neither, really, did she.

He learned her habits the way a person learns the habits of someone they've decided to keep. He knew when she was lying about being tired. He knew that Troy preferred shade over sun and would not admit it. He knew how she took her tea, and which of her scars she would talk about and which she would not.

To him, every one of those small things became something worth keeping.

To Aya, they were simply what a person learns about a friend.

He loved her the other way, the way she never returned. When he finally told her so, she did not laugh, and she did not make it smaller than it was. She told him the truth instead, which cost her more than a lie would have and gave him less comfort than one might have too.

Her heart already belonged to someone who was not there.

He tried to stay close after that. She tried to make the staying easier on him. Neither of them managed it. Eventually she began to leave without waiting for him some mornings, not because she had stopped caring what happened to him, but because she had started to understand that caring loudly, after a confession like his, was its own kind of cruelty.

To her, in the end, he had been a very close friend. Nothing more. There were others like him in the years before and the years after — people she had loved as family and never as anything else. None of them had been the one she was still waiting for.

She never learned, afterward, how far his grief would carry him, or what he would eventually be willing to walk into in order to stop feeling it. To her he simply became another person she had once loved without knowing quite how much of him she was letting go.

Troy watched her scratch at a river's edge some evenings, watched the water gather around her ankles and go still, and never once asked when she planned to stop waiting.

He had followed her through death itself. He already knew the answer.

She never would.
