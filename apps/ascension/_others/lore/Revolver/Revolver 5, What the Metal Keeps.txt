# Revolver 5, What the Metal Keeps

Deadeye was the only one who still cleaned his weapon before breakfast, and the others had long since stopped commenting on it out loud, though Tech called it obsessive when he thought no one was listening and Swift called it old-fashioned to his face. Euphoria had once asked, half-joking, whether he polished the rifle because he trusted it more than people. He'd looked at her for a long moment before answering.

"Yes."

She laughed. He didn't.

That morning the safehouse smelled of gun oil and burnt toast, and Deadeye sat at the kitchen table with the rifle broken into its parts in front of him when Dagger came to the doorway.

"You knew Ghost was alive."

"No."

"You think he is?"

"No."

"Then why are you still preparing for him?"

Deadeye ran a cloth through the bolt without looking up. "Because you're preparing for him."

"That's not an answer."

"It is." He glanced up, and saw in the boy's face a look he recognized from Euphoria — the narrowed eyes, the careful stillness of someone deciding whether to press further. He almost smiled at that. "Sit."

Dagger sat.

"When you expect someone to come through a door," Deadeye said, setting the bolt on the cloth, "you don't prepare for the person you're hoping walks through it."

"Who do you prepare for?"

"Whoever does," he said, and went back to the rifle, and let the silence finish the lesson the way silence usually did with this particular student — either he'd understand eventually, or he wouldn't, and either way the weapon still needed cleaning.

---

His first rifle had belonged to someone else, and he'd been twelve years old the first time he held it — not at a range, not under instruction, no paper target or marked distance or anyone correcting his stance. Only a window, a road, and a man walking toward the house where his family was hiding.

The rifle was too heavy for his hands. Someone whispered behind him. *Wait.* He waited. The man came closer — a hundred meters, eighty, sixty — close enough that Deadeye could see the cigarette between his fingers. Forty. Thirty. The man stopped. His finger tightened on the trigger.

*Not yet.*

He waited. The cigarette fell. The man's head turned toward the sound of it, and Deadeye fired, and the rifle kicked hard into his shoulder, and the man dropped out of the window frame. Nobody celebrated. Someone closed the curtains. They stayed inside until night fell around them like something finally exhaling.

That was how precision entered his life — not as sport, not as pride, but as the narrow space between death and the people standing behind him. Years later he'd forget the man's face entirely. He never forgot the cigarette, the tiny orange point burning in the dark, or the fact that the human body had proven far easier to lose track of than the distance had.

---

Ghost handed him a rifle the day he recruited him, and Deadeye was already older than most of the others by then.

"Good weapon."

"You're not going to ask where it came from?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you expect me to."

Ghost looked at him for a beat. Deadeye looked back and pointed at a scratch on the receiver — three millimeters, maybe four.

"Your weapon is damaged."

"Not enough to matter."

"It will."

Ghost said nothing.

"Metal remembers," Deadeye said, and Ghost smiled at that, and that was the first time Deadeye understood why the man had gone to the trouble of gathering all of them in the first place. He hadn't recruited people because they were perfect. He'd recruited people who'd already learned how to survive being imperfect, which was a very different skill, and a much rarer one.

The Revolver became a machine built entirely out of incompatible parts. It shouldn't have worked. It did anyway.

---

The first time he met Maze, Maze asked how many bullets he carried.

"Enough."

"That's not a number."

"It wasn't a question that deserved one."

Maze smiled. "I like you."

"I don't care."

"I know."

They worked an extraction together in Prague not long after — Maze navigating the building, Deadeye covering him, both of them reaching the fourth floor before Maze stopped cold.

"Problem?"

"Three."

"People?"

"Doors."

Deadeye looked down the corridor. "Two."

"There are three."

"The third isn't real."

Maze stared at him, then laughed, because the third door turned out to be painted onto the wall — a test, and Deadeye had passed it without knowing it was being administered. He disliked Maze immediately after that. He trusted him shortly afterward, and never quite worked out how those two things had ended up occupying the same space in his head at once.

---

In the present, Deadeye sat in the observation room while Tech replayed the recovered footage — Ghost's face never once appearing in it, only movement, a shoulder, a hand, a figure crossing a corridor.

"You see it?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"Someone wants us to think that's Ghost."

"That's what I thought." Tech looked at him sideways. "Then why show me?"

"Because you notice things."

Deadeye watched it again. The figure paused beside a doorway, three seconds, moved, and disappeared. "Frame thirty-two." Tech paused it there. Deadeye pointed. "There."

A reflection in the glass — not Ghost. A second figure.

"Can you clean it?"

"Already tried."

"Then stop."

"You don't want to know?"

"I want to know. Knowing something and knowing what to do with it are different."

"That's a very Deadeye answer," Tech said, and Deadeye ignored him, studying the frozen image instead. The second figure had one hand against the wall, a sleeve dark except for a thin strip of pale material showing at the wrist. He knew that pattern from somewhere — not recently, not in the field, but from an old photograph — and left without explaining any of it.

Tech followed him into the hall. "What did you see?"

"Nothing."

"You're a terrible liar."

He stopped walking. Tech almost ran into his back. "Don't follow me."

"Why?"

"Because I don't want you to," he said, and this time, for once, Tech didn't.

---

The photograph came from a file that shouldn't have still existed, and he kept it in a metal box under his bed that none of the others knew about. It showed the team years earlier — Ghost in the center, Swift looking away from the camera, Virtuoso mid-smile, Euphoria's hand resting on Maze's shoulder, Tech holding two fingers up behind Disposer's head. Deadeye stood at the edge of the frame. Maze stood slightly apart from everyone else, and that was the detail Deadeye had never been able to shake — not the expression on anyone's face, but the distance Maze had left between himself and the rest of them, as if some part of him had already been rehearsing an exit.

Three days after that photograph, Maze had asked him a strange question while Deadeye cleaned his rifle.

"How long does it take you to decide whether someone's dangerous?"

"Depends."

"On?"

"Whether I need them alive."

"You always answer like that."

"Because you always ask badly."

Maze had leaned against the table. "Suppose you didn't."

"Didn't what?"

"Need them alive."

Deadeye had looked at him and gotten nothing further — no follow-up, no explanation, just a strange question left hanging in the air between two men who were supposed to trust each other. He never reported it. Not because he trusted Maze particularly, but because a strange question wasn't evidence of anything. It was only a strange question. That was what he'd told himself at the time, and had gone on telling himself for years afterward, until the telling itself started to feel like the thing worth questioning.

---

After the betrayal, everyone else searched their memories for signs they'd missed. Deadeye searched records instead — records didn't change their expressions under questioning, didn't deny remembering, didn't get offended. They just held timestamps, locations, names, distances, the raw material a timeline could be built from without anyone's feelings getting in the way.

Maze had accessed three systems before the betrayal. One belonged to Tech. One belonged to Ghost. The third was an old operational archive concerning a mission from twelve years back. Deadeye opened it and recognized the location before he'd finished reading the header — he'd been there once, years ago, on a mission marked complete with no team casualties and one civilian death, which was the kind of line item that used to mean almost nothing to him.

He opened the photographs one at a time. The fourth one stopped him: a burned vehicle beside a roadside ditch, a body under a sheet, the sheet folded back slightly at one corner to reveal a small hand.

He stared at it for a long time before he closed the file. He remembered the mission. He remembered the vehicle, the smell of wet earth, standing forty meters back while Disposer worked. He did not remember the child. That frightened him — not the gap in the memory itself, but the fact that the thing missing from it had once been a person.

---

Dagger found him in the shooting room that evening, three targets down range, each one already carrying a hole through the forehead.

"Can you teach me?"

"No."

"Why?"

"You don't need this."

"I do."

"Everyone says that."

"I need to protect myself."

"Then learn to run."

"I thought you hated running."

"I hate being shot," Deadeye said, checking the chamber. "How do you decide when to pull the trigger?"

"I don't. The decision happens before."

"Then how do you know?"

"By knowing what happens after."

Dagger went quiet. Deadeye handed him the rifle. "Hold it." He did. "Too tight." He loosened his grip. "Better." He raised it, and Deadeye adjusted his elbow. "Don't aim at the center."

"Why?"

"Because people aren't targets," Deadeye said, and took the rifle back, and neither of them spoke for a moment before Dagger asked the question that had probably been sitting behind his teeth the whole time.

"Did you ever shoot someone you didn't think deserved it?"

The question stayed in the room between them longer than it should have.

"Yes."

"Who?"

He put the rifle back in its case. "I don't remember."

Dagger knew that was a lie. So did Deadeye. Neither of them corrected it, and there was something almost merciful in leaving it that way.

---

The next morning he found a tiny white line near the receiver of his rifle — paint, and he'd cleaned that exact part the night before, which meant someone had touched the weapon recently enough to leave a mark without doing any real damage. Just enough to leave a trace, the way someone leaves a trace on purpose when they want you to know they were there.

He checked the safehouse cameras. Nothing. Tech had been upstairs, Swift outside, Euphoria with Dagger, Virtuoso gone, Disposer not yet back. He looked at the mark again, then at the door, and could have reported it. Instead he wiped it clean — the cloth came back white — folded it into his pocket, and told no one.

Another small thing that could have been evidence, filed away instead as only a suspicion. He was starting to understand how betrayal actually survived in a place like this — not through some single grand secret, but through a long accumulation of ordinary decisions. A person notices something strange and says nothing. Notices another thing, says nothing again. And one day the accumulated silence has quietly become a wall nobody remembers building.

---

That night he went back to the old archive. The child was still there in the fourth photograph, and this time he enlarged it — the sheet, the hand, the mud, and then the background, where a figure stood beside the burned vehicle. Disposer. And behind him, mostly out of frame, someone else.

He enlarged it further. The face stayed obscured. The coat didn't.

Maze's.

He sat back and didn't breathe for several seconds. He could tell the others. He could tell Ghost, except Ghost was gone. He could tell Dagger, except Dagger was already carrying more than enough. He could tell Tech, except Tech would ask questions, and questions led to answers, and answers led somewhere Deadeye didn't have a map for.

For most of his life, uncertainty had been a distance he could measure — two hundred meters, a crosswind, a heartbeat, a trigger, variables that always ended somewhere solid. This didn't end anywhere. He looked at the photograph one last time until the child's hand stopped being a hand at all and became only pixels arranged in a shape, and then he shut the screen down and cleaned the rifle again in the dark. Once. Twice. A third time, though there was no dirt left on it to justify the effort.

Somewhere underneath the cleaning sat a thought he wouldn't let himself finish — that maybe patience hadn't made him careful so much as it had taught him exactly how long he could look at a person before they stopped being a person and became a shape, a distance, an angle, a problem with a solution attached to it.

He locked the rifle back in its case. Checked the lock. Checked it again. Footsteps passed outside the door, and his hand went to the weapon before he'd consciously decided it should, and the footsteps kept going down the hall without stopping.

He didn't open the door. He couldn't say, sitting there in the dark with his hand still resting on the case, whether the danger he was listening for was out there in the corridor, or whether by now he'd already carried it inside the room with him.
