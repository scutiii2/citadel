# The Girl Who Learned What Worship Was For

The third man to attempt the ritual convulsed for eleven seconds and then simply stopped, the way a lamp stops when the oil runs out — not dramatically, just an absence where a moment ago there had been a flame.

His name was Torvin Ashe, and he had spent four years believing himself devoted enough to survive what the first two men hadn't. He had fasted. He had given away land. He had knelt in temples built in a woman's honor long after his knees had stopped forgiving him for it. When his body finally failed on the altar stone, the priests around him did not weep so much as make notes, because grief, at this stage of the enterprise, had already been budgeted for.

Mei watched from across the chamber, arms folded, and said nothing for a long moment.

Then she said, "Try the next one differently. He held his breath through all of it. Tell him not to."

It was not cruelty. It rarely was, with her. It was closer to the tone of a craftsman troubleshooting a tool that kept breaking in the same predictable place, and if that tone unsettled the priests more than open malice would have, none of them ever said so to her directly.

To understand how a girl becomes someone capable of watching a man die on an altar built for her and immediately begin solving the problem, it helps to go back to the forest where she was found, small and unafraid, in wreckage that should have killed anything human.

---

The Enchants who found the crater expected humans. They had found humans before, fallen from the sky in cruder vessels, frightened and half-starved and grateful. What they found instead was a child sitting upright in the ruin of her pod, entirely unhurt, looking back at them with the mild curiosity of someone waiting to be told what happened next.

The oldest among them felt it before the youngest did — a wrongness in her that had nothing to do with malice and everything to do with scale. Ordinary magic, the kind that moved through an Enchant's blood after generations of inherited bond, had a texture the oldest could recognize with their eyes closed. What moved through this child had no texture at all. It simply was her, the way water is wet, with no separation between the girl and the force running through her.

They debated what to do with her the way frightened people debate anything — badly, and at length, and never quite reaching consensus. Some wanted to study her. Some wanted her destroyed before whatever slept inside her woke fully. In the end they did neither, because doing either required someone to walk toward her first, and for three full days, nobody did.

She solved that problem herself, eventually, by walking into their camp uninvited and asking, without any apparent awareness that the question might unsettle anyone further, why everyone kept hiding from her when she could see exactly where they were standing.

An elder answered, from a safe distance, that she was not what she appeared to be.

"I know," the girl said. "I don't know what I am either. Is that supposed to be frightening?"

Nobody answered her. She shrugged, the particular shrug of a child who has decided the adults in the room are being unreasonable and doesn't intend to waste more energy on the argument, and went looking for somewhere else to be a person.

They named her Mei before they worked up the nerve to ask her whether she already had a name. She did not correct them. It is possible she didn't have one to offer.

---

She left the Enchants within a year, drawn — by instinct or boredom, the record does not distinguish — toward the human settlements where the Evolved lived closest to ordinary people. Humans did not carry the old instinct the Enchants had for recognizing wrongness in a bloodline; to them, Mei was simply a remarkable child, the kind every generation produces one or two of, until the things she did stopped fitting inside that explanation.

She lifted what should have required ten Evolved men and did not appear to notice the effort. She stopped a wall from finishing its collapse onto a group of children with one raised hand and looked mildly surprised that anyone found this remarkable. She did not hide any of it, because it never occurred to her that hiding was a thing people in her position generally chose to do.

Word travels faster than caution. An Evolved soldier who had watched her hold up a falling roof told three others. A woman whose dying orchard she'd restored in an afternoon told her whole village. A child who'd watched her call rain down over cracked, drought-starved fields told anyone who would listen, for the rest of a very long life, and was believed less each time she told it, right up until the year everyone finally understood she'd been telling the truth all along.

They started asking her for things. She gave them, because giving cost her nothing she could measure and made people around her happier than she'd previously understood people were capable of being. For a while, that was the whole of it — a girl who liked being useful, mistaking usefulness for the entire shape of what she was.

---

She noticed the pattern the way most discoveries happen — sideways, while looking for something else entirely.

A village had begun gathering at dusk to give thanks for a harvest she'd saved, and during one such gathering, kneeling and murmuring and genuinely believing, for reasons she hadn't asked them to supply, that she could hear them and would keep them safe if they only trusted her enough — she felt something answer that had nothing to do with the wheat she'd grown or the rain she'd called. It moved toward her the way heat moves toward an open door, and it did not come from the ground, or from any living thing nearby.

She tested it, carefully, the way she tested everything — by asking for more of the same and seeing whether more of the same arrived. She asked a second village to build her a shrine. She asked a third to gather and simply believe, out loud, together, that she was capable of everything they hoped she was. Each time, the answering force grew a little stronger, a little easier to feel, until she stopped needing to ask for it consciously and started simply noticing it arrive whenever enough people, in enough rooms, decided together that she was worth trusting entirely.

She understood, before she had a name for it, that this was not magic in any sense the Enchants would have recognized, and not strength in any sense the Evolved possessed. It came from somewhere she had never seen and could not point to, and it grew in direct proportion to how completely other people surrendered their fear to her.

They had already given her a name for what she was, long before she found the right one for the power itself. They called her their God.

She let them.

Not, in fairness to her, out of vanity — she never once claimed to be flawless, and would have been genuinely confused by anyone who accused her of pretending otherwise. She let them because the name was, as far as she could tell, simply accurate to what was happening, and Mei had never been a person who saw much use in refusing an accurate description of herself.

---

What she wanted, once she understood the shape of the power, was more of it, and the fastest way to get more of anything, she'd already learned from watching farmers manage crops, was to plant more of whatever produced it.

She could not hand her own power to another person directly — she tried, in the earliest attempts, and the results convinced her quickly that whatever this force was, it required a soul to reach for it on its own rather than simply receive it as a gift. So she built rituals instead. She taught devotion as a discipline, the way an instructor teaches a sword stance, refining it each time a student failed, treating each failure as data rather than as tragedy, which was either the most honest way to survive what she was doing or the first sign of something in her that had already stopped being entirely a girl.

Torvin Ashe was neither the first nor the last to die on her altar stone. He was, however, adjacent to the first true success — a woman named Sarei Voss, who followed him onto the same stone four days later, having been told, on Mei's specific instruction, to breathe through the pain rather than hold against it.

Sarei did not die. She screamed for eleven minutes, which those present later swore felt considerably longer, and when it ended, something in her had changed at a level deeper than skin. She stood on legs that no longer belonged entirely to a human body, looked at her own hands as if they'd been swapped out overnight for someone else's, and asked, in a voice still raw from screaming, what she was now.

"Divine," Mei told her, and the word had never existed with that meaning before the moment she said it.

---

The system that grew from Sarei Voss's survival did not require cruelty to sustain itself, which made it, in the long run, considerably more dangerous than if it had. Every Divine Ascendant who survived the ritual became not merely a follower but a second thread reaching toward whatever lay beyond Earth, and every thread widened the channel a little further, and the widened channel made the next Ascension a little more survivable, which meant more attempts, which meant more threads.

Temples rose in places that had never needed temples before. Kings who had spent their reigns fighting each other over grain and borders sent envoys asking for her blessing instead, having quietly concluded that her favor was worth more than the grain. Evolved warriors who had spent their lives being told they were the strongest thing a human body could become knelt in her name and discovered they meant it. Even a handful of Enchants — the ones young enough not to remember the fear their elders had felt in that first forest, or old enough to have finally decided fear had cost their people more than curiosity ever would — began, quietly, to allow that she might be something worth aligning with rather than something to be watched from a safe distance.

Not everyone kneeled. There were always some — among Enchants especially, whose collective memory ran longer and colder than anyone else's — who watched the temples multiply and the kings bow and said, to whoever would listen, that no single being should be allowed to become this necessary to this many people at once. They were rarely wrong. They were also, by then, rarely in a position to be listened to.

Mei herself changed slower than her religion did, which is perhaps the most honest thing that can be said of her. She still remembered being a child who had simply wanted people to stop being afraid of her. She still, on nights when the temples were quiet and the priests had gone to sleep, wondered — in a way she never let her Divine Ascendants see — where exactly she had come from, and whether the power moving through her belonged to her at all, or was only borrowed from something enormous and distant that had never once, in all the years since her pod struck the ground, bothered to explain itself.

She had a name for whatever that distant thing was, though she had chosen it herself, without proof, the same practical way she chose everything: the Creator. She did not know if the name was accurate. She did not know if the being it described was watching her build an empire out of borrowed devotion, or had never noticed her at all, or was even still capable of noticing anything, wherever it was.

She only knew that the temples kept multiplying, and the power kept answering, and somewhere above all of it — beyond the sky her pod had fallen through, beyond the reach of every Divine Ascendant she had ever made — there was a source she had still never once managed to touch directly.

So she kept building the only bridge she knew how to build, one convert, one shrine, one ritual at a time, reaching upward with both hands toward something that had never yet reached back.
