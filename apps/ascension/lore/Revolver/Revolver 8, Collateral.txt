# COLLATERAL

There were eight of them in the room, and Dagger remembered every one of them putting him on the floor at least once that year.

Swift moved first — a blur across the training floor, the wooden blade stopping less than an inch from his throat before he'd finished raising his arm to block it. *"Dead,"* she said, and stepped back like the verdict cost her nothing.

Dagger breathed through his teeth and didn't argue, because arguing had never once worked.

Across the room, Deadeye lowered his rifle without having fired it yet. *"You were dead before Swift moved."*

"I know."

"Then why did you move?"

"Because I thought I could beat Swift."

Deadeye gave the smallest possible nod, the kind that cost him nothing and told Dagger everything. *"That was your second mistake."*

Dagger frowned. "What's the first?"

"Thinking you were fighting Swift."

The rifle cracked before Dagger had time to be afraid of it, and he dropped a half-second late, the bullet punching into the wooden target that had been standing exactly where his head was. Tech didn't look up from the machine he was dismantling. Virtuoso laughed from somewhere Dagger couldn't see and didn't bother locating. Maze had rearranged the room three times that morning without telling anyone, the way he did most mornings, as if the compound itself needed to learn not to trust its own floor plan.

Euphoria had spent the week before teaching him to read a lie in the half-second of hesitation before it was told. Disposer had taught him how to leave a room with no proof he'd been in it. Swift had taught him, over and over, in the specific language of a wooden blade at his throat, that speed meant nothing against someone who already knew where he'd be before he moved there.

Eight teachers. Eight different ways to die, demonstrated patiently, repeatedly, until dying stopped being a surprise and started being a Tuesday.

Dagger hated all of them for it. That was the part he never told anyone — hatred was why he kept coming back, because a boy with nothing left to protect needed somewhere to put the feeling that used to be grief.

He never learned there was a ninth teacher. He never saw him, not directly — sometimes a shape behind the observation glass, sometimes a silhouette on the roofline against a sky too bright to look at directly, sometimes nothing at all except the specific quality of a darkness at the edge of the compound where the cameras had no jurisdiction. Ghost watched him fall. Watched him rise. Watched him fail in the small, private ways nobody else in the room had patience to notice.

Every time Dagger was certain no one was watching, Ghost watched a little longer than that.

He told himself it was because he didn't want the boy to become what he'd become. He wanted him better. That was the difference, or that was the sentence Ghost repeated to himself often enough that it had started to sound less like a fact and more like a debt he was trying to pay down.

---

The first year was mostly pain. The second turned into humiliation. By the third, humiliation had gone quiet and routine, the way any wound does once the body stops treating it as news.

He took Swift's speed and Deadeye's patience and Tech's machines and Euphoria's read on a liar's silence and Virtuoso's fingers, quick enough to lift a wallet or a key or an alibi. Maze taught him to turn confusion into a weapon; Disposer taught him to erase the evidence that violence had ever visited a room. And underneath every separate lesson sat the same one, repeated in eight different accents: survival wasn't about strength. It was about making sure the other person ran out of choices before you did.

Ghost had learned that lesson better than any of them, which was probably why he'd made himself impossible to see. Dagger had caught one glimpse of him in three years — a silhouette at the end of a corridor, black coat, pale mask, gone before his feet had finished deciding to move toward it. He'd asked the others who it was. Nobody answered, and the silence sat there long enough to become an answer of its own.

---

Years folded into each other the way training does — Dagger got faster, then quieter, then something closer to patient. Eventually he stopped asking whether he could win a given fight and started asking what winning would cost him, and that was the exact point at which Ghost started paying real attention, because it was the question Ghost had forgotten how to ask himself somewhere along the way.

He watched, once, from a darkened rooftop, as Dagger finished an assignment alone — three targets, no casualties, no alarms, nothing left behind that a cleanup crew would ever need to touch. Afterward Dagger stood under a streetlamp looking at a smear of blood on his glove that wasn't his and wasn't anyone's, because he hadn't needed to kill to get it there.

Ghost looked down at him and smiled for the first time in longer than he could count.

*"Good,"* he said, quiet enough that it shouldn't have carried. Dagger looked up anyway. By then Ghost was already gone.

---

The truth about his parents didn't reach him until he was old enough to survive it — or old enough, at least, that the people keeping it from him decided the arithmetic had changed.

He'd spent years on the ordinary lie people tell themselves when there's no better one available: an accident, a bad night, wrong place, wrong time, the kind of sentence that closes a door instead of opening one.

Then Maze found the file. Three photographs — a street, a vehicle, two bodies — and a final page with a single name typed at the bottom of it.

Ghost.

Dagger didn't scream. He didn't cry. He closed the file the way you close a door you already know you're going to have to walk back through, and then he walked.

He found Ghost waiting for him beneath an abandoned railway station, standing beside a vending machine that had been dead longer than either of them had been in this line of work.

"You knew," Dagger said.

Ghost said nothing.

"You killed them."

"Yes." The word came so evenly that Dagger nearly lowered the weapon on reflex — nearly, and then didn't.

"Why?"

"There was no other way." Ghost's eyes went to the tracks instead of to him.

"There is always another way."

"Not for them."

"They were innocent."

"I know," Ghost said, and that landed worse than any excuse would have, because an excuse at least gives you somewhere to aim the anger.

Dagger closed the distance between them by a step. "They were just walking through that area."

"Yes."

"They weren't involved."

"No."

"They weren't your enemies."

"No."

"Then why did you kill them?"

Ghost finally looked at him — no anger in it, no visible regret either, just a stillness that Dagger would spend years trying to name. "Because they were going to die."

"Everyone dies."

"Not like that." Ghost gestured toward the empty tracks, toward a night neither of them could undo by describing it. "There was an operation running through the district that evening. Weapons moving, and the men moving them already hunted by people worse than me. Your parents walked into the middle of it without knowing what they were walking into."

"You could have moved them."

"I tried."

"You could have warned them."

"I did."

"Then why?"

"Because the moment they stepped onto that street, the ending had already been decided. I only carried it the rest of the way."

Dagger shook his head. "That's convenient."

"No." Ghost took a step closer, close enough that the mask's breathing vents were the only sound between them for a second. "Convenient would have been telling myself I'd had a choice."

The blade in Dagger's hand had started to shake without his permission. "You murdered them."

"I made their fate true."

The silence that followed lasted longer than any of the sentences that had come before it.

"They were always going to be found dead," Ghost said finally. "I only became the one who made sure of it."

"You don't get to decide someone's fate."

Something moved at the edge of Ghost's mouth that might have been a smile in a kinder light. "Neither did I."

"Then who did?"

He didn't answer that directly — just looked at Dagger for a while, the way a man looks at a door he isn't sure he's allowed to open yet. "You'll understand eventually."

"I will never understand you."

"You will." Ghost started to walk past him. "And when you do, you'll thank me."

Dagger turned to follow the words with his whole body. "For killing my parents?"

Ghost stopped, but didn't turn around. "For making sure you became someone who could survive losing them."

"I would rather have had them."

For a moment Ghost's voice lost whatever had been holding it steady. "So would I."

And there — for the first time, in the half-second before the mask turned fully away — Dagger saw something behind it that wasn't guilt. It was worse than guilt. It had the shape of a man who still believed, completely, that he'd been right.

---

After that night Dagger trained harder than he had in three years combined, and Ghost kept watching, the way you watch a fire you started and can't decide whether to put out.

Faster. Colder. Increasingly fluent in the private grammar of every member of the team, until he could read a room the way Euphoria did and clear one the way Disposer did without either of them standing beside him to check his work.

But something else had shifted too, something none of his teachers had put there on purpose. He stopped trusting his own victories. A clean mission left a residue in him now — not pride, something closer to suspicion, as if every dead enemy were less a resolution than a rehearsal for something worse still coming.

One night, in the rain, he confronted Ghost again. "What are you training me for?"

"Me," Ghost said.

Dagger didn't follow. "What?"

"I'm training you to defeat me."

Ghost reached up and removed the mask before Dagger could ask him to explain that, and underneath it he looked older than Dagger had ever pictured him — not in the skin, but somewhere behind the eyes, like a man who'd been awake for more years than his face had any record of.

"One day I'm going to forget why I started," Ghost said.

"You already have."

A small, unbothered smile. "Perhaps."

"Then why keep going?"

"Because someone has to."

Dagger lowered his weapon a few inches, not in surrender, in calculation. "No. Someone has to stop you."

Ghost nodded once, like a man agreeing to his own sentencing. "Exactly."

---

Ghost took him somewhere the team had never brought him — a cemetery with no name worth remembering, the kind of forgotten ground where rain had worn the letters off half the headstones and nobody had come to mourn the other half in years.

He stopped in front of a grave with no name left on it at all.

For a while neither of them spoke, and the quiet did more work than a speech would have. Then Ghost crouched, brushed a decade of moss off a corner of the stone with two fingers, and said, without looking up, "Every one of them was someone's villain, before they became a lesson."

Dagger didn't answer that. He didn't have an answer ready.

"I used to think I was ending something," Ghost said, standing again, looking not at Dagger but at the rows of graves stretching past him into the dark. "I killed the men who made this compound necessary. All of them, eventually. And when the last one was gone, I stood in a very quiet city and understood I'd made a mistake I couldn't take back."

"What mistake."

Ghost didn't explain it the way a lecture would have. He just walked, slowly, between two rows of stones, and let Dagger follow him into the rest of it at his own pace. "Ask Swift sometime why she trains the way she does. Ask Deadeye what he was, before this. Every one of them became what they are because something worse existed first, and had to be answered."

Dagger looked at the graves instead of at him. "And you killed the thing they were answering."

"I did."

"So what happens to people who don't have anything left to answer?"

Ghost stopped walking. For a long moment the only sound was rain finding its way through a gap in the cemetery's iron gate. "They start looking for something to fight anyway. Some of them find it in each other. Some of them go looking for a monster, and when they can't find one—"

"They become one."

Ghost didn't confirm it out loud. He didn't have to. The silence that followed was its own answer, and it was worse than anything he could have said.

"That's when you became one," Dagger said.

"I killed them all," Ghost said, and there was no pride in it, and no apology either — just a man reporting a fact he'd stopped being able to feel anything about.

"And you're proud of that?"

"No."

"Then why say it like a fact instead of a confession?"

"Because I stopped being sure which one it was, a long time before you were old enough to ask me."

He looked at his own hands for a moment, turning them over as if checking them for something that should have been visible by now.

"You could have stopped," Dagger said.

"I tried."

Ghost looked up at him, and for once there was nothing performative in it. "That's why you're here."

---

Dagger understood it then, all at once, the way a bone understands a break before the pain arrives to explain it.

He had never been a student. He'd been a replacement being assembled one lesson, one wound, one failure at a time — a weapon Ghost had spent years quietly building to be capable of ending him.

A stronger Ghost. A better one. Maybe one with enough conscience left intact to actually stop, where the first one hadn't been able to.

That was the part that frightened him. Not that Ghost wanted him dead.

That Ghost wanted him to survive it.

---

The last confrontation happened underground, in a station the city had forgotten it owned — rusted rails, dead signal lights, no witnesses and no team and no one left to record which version of the story would end up being true.

Ghost wore the mask. Dagger held the blade.

"You knew this would happen."

"Yes."

"You wanted me to come."

"Yes."

"You killed people so I'd hate you." A pause, and then, quieter, worse: "You killed my parents so I'd hate you most of all."

Ghost said nothing to either accusation, and the nothing was its own kind of confession.

"You made me stronger," Dagger said.

"I had to."

"You made me into this."

Ghost looked at him for a long moment before he answered. "No. I showed you what was already there."

They moved before either of them decided to.

Swift's speed lived in Dagger's feet now. Deadeye's patience sat behind his eyes, waiting for an opening instead of chasing one. Tech's precision, Euphoria's misdirection, Virtuoso's sleight, Maze's chaos, Disposer's clean economy of motion — eight teachers moving through one body, and beneath all of them, underneath every trained reflex, was the shape of the ninth teacher he'd never officially had.

They fought until there was almost nothing recognizable left of the fight itself. Dagger found the opening first. He struck once — Ghost staggered — and struck again, and the mask split down one side with a sound like something finally being allowed to break.

Ghost went down.

Dagger stood over him with his chest working hard enough to hurt, and for several seconds neither of them moved at all. Then Ghost laughed, quietly, almost kindly, the way a man laughs at a joke that's finally landed after years of bad timing.

"You did it."

Dagger stared down at him. "What?"

"You became stronger than me."

"No," Dagger said, and the word came out steadier than he felt.

"Yes." Ghost looked up at him without fear in it, only something that might have been relief wearing fear's old coat. "You'll see."

"I'm not you."

"Not yet."

Dagger drove the blade down before either of them could say anything else, and the station swallowed the sound whole. Ghost fell through the gap in the broken platform into the dark below it, and the dark didn't give him back.

Dagger stood alone in the quiet afterward, and for the first time in his life there was no Ghost anywhere at the edge of it.

He told himself, standing there, that he'd done something clean. Ended the man who'd ended his parents. Proven, in the only language this life had ever offered him, that strength didn't have to calcify into cruelty — that a person could come out the other side of all this still recognizably human.

At least, that was what he believed, standing in the rusted quiet with a blade that hadn't stopped shaking yet.

---

No one ever found Ghost's body. It didn't matter. Everyone believed he was dead, and belief, in the Revolver, had always done the job a body was supposed to do.

They mourned the way they always had — quietly, in six or seven private ways nobody compared notes on. Dagger never told them the rest of it. Never told them Ghost had wanted this, had built toward it for years the way another man might build toward retirement. Never told them that in the last second before the blade came down, Ghost hadn't looked afraid.

He'd looked like a man setting something down he'd been carrying too long.

There had been eight of them, and only eight — Swift, Deadeye, Tech, Euphoria, Virtuoso, Maze, Disposer, Ghost, each one a chamber in the same weapon, each one loaded once and left to wait its turn. Dagger had learned to recite the names before he understood what they meant, the way a boy learns a chant before he's told what it's for.

Two of those chambers were empty now.

Maze's had emptied first, in a room none of them spoke about, for a betrayal the file had proven but never explained — and it had emptied a second time in the sense that mattered, the night Tech found a reflection in a cracked window and understood, without anyone saying it aloud, that Ghost had been the one who closed that chamber for good.

Ghost's had emptied second, tonight, underground, with no one watching but the two men it happened to.

Dagger stood over the gap in the platform where the fall had swallowed everything, and did not know, standing there, which of the two seats he'd just inherited.

He had trained under Ghost's hand for years, absorbed his patience and his precision and the particular stillness he wore like a second mask, until the two of them moved through the world in postures close enough to be mistaken for each other from a distance. That was one kind of replacement — the kind everyone would assume, later, when they saw what he'd become.

But there was a second kind, quieter, that nobody would ever be in a position to notice. Maze had died for keeping something from the people who trusted him. Dagger walked back to the safe house that night already deciding what he would keep from them too — that Ghost had wanted this, had built toward it on purpose, had looked almost grateful in the second before the blade came down. He would carry that the way Maze had once carried coordinates and schedules, sealed, useful to no one but himself, waiting for a moment he told himself would never come.

And there was a third possibility neither of the other two accounted for, one that didn't fit into a single chamber no matter which name got written on it. Everything that had happened to the eight of them — Maze's betrayal, the warehouse, the year of searching, Ghost's disappearance and the thing he became instead — had passed through Dagger like current through a wire, until every lesson the original seven had ever taught him stopped belonging to them individually and started belonging only to him. Swift's speed. Deadeye's patience. Tech's precision. Euphoria's read on a lie. Virtuoso's hands. Maze's gift for turning a room against itself. Disposer's talent for leaving nothing behind. He had not replaced any one of them. He had absorbed the shape of all of them, the way a weapon doesn't remember which hand loaded which bullet — it only knows how to fire.

A revolver doesn't announce which chamber is loaded, and it doesn't announce whether it's a chamber at all, or the whole mechanism turning around one. It only fires, and afterward there is no way to ask it what it meant to be.

Dagger went back to the team. Trained. Fought. Took the assignments that came his way and killed the people the assignments required him to kill. Somewhere in the months after, without ever marking the exact night it happened, he began killing people the assignment hadn't strictly required — a shift so small he could tell himself, each time, that it wasn't a pattern yet.

One night he stood over a man who'd already surrendered, weapon on the ground, both hands raised, voice cracking around a single word. *"Please."*

Something in Dagger, low and patient, said *finish it* in a voice that didn't belong to anyone he could name — or belonged, perhaps, to all seven of them at once, which was worse.

He raised the blade. And for one bad second he heard Ghost instead of himself: *there would be no heroes, if there were never any villains to answer.*

Dagger froze. Then he lowered the weapon, and the man ran, and Dagger let him go and told himself that was proof — that he was different, that Ghost had been wrong about him.

The man he'd spared would go on, in a city Dagger would never visit, to hurt someone who hadn't done anything to deserve it. Dagger would hear about it eventually, secondhand, the way you hear about weather in a country you used to live in. And when he did, he wouldn't think about the stranger. He'd think about Ghost, and about how easy it had been, for one clean second under a streetlamp, to understand exactly why Ghost had once believed himself right.

He never let himself call that understanding by its name. He told himself, instead, that his fate was still his own to write.

He believed that for longer than was reasonable.

Because the man he'd buried beneath the abandoned platform hadn't stayed buried. He'd only stopped being Ghost — the mask gone, the name retired along with it, something in him rearranged by the fall into a shape that hadn't finished deciding what it wanted yet.

Somewhere past the edges of the Revolver's maps, a man with no name began to walk again.

Dagger would not learn, for a long while yet, what the world had started calling him.

**The Collector.**

He only knew, standing alone in that dead station, that something in the weapon he'd been made inside of had changed shape, and that he could no longer tell, from where he stood, whether he was the bullet that had just been fired, or the hand that had just finished pulling the trigger, or the gun itself, closing around an empty chamber that had never really been empty at all.

The Revolver never told him which.

It only kept turning.
