# Revolver 2, What the Hand Remembers

The first thing Virtuoso stole from the Revolver was a key, and nobody noticed, which was the entire point of stealing anything.

It was brass, old enough that the teeth had gone soft at the edges, lifted from a ring hanging beside Ghost's coat in the safehouse corridor. He took it while passing through with two cups of coffee, turned the ring once around his finger, and dropped it back exactly where he'd found it. The key opened nothing important. At least, that was what he told himself.

Three days later he returned it anyway. Ghost never asked where it had been, and that bothered Virtuoso more than an accusation would have.

Years afterward, hunting Ghost through a city that no longer felt like it belonged to any of them, Virtuoso would remember that key every time he found himself holding something he had no business touching. He was holding such a thing now.

The building had been abandoned for six years, though someone had swept the corridor outside the third floor recently enough that the dust lines still showed the broom strokes. Everywhere else, gray ridges. Near the stairwell, fresh footprints — small, adult, cheap rubber soles worn unevenly at the heel.

He crouched beside them for a moment before looking at the locked door across the hall.

The instruction had been simple: get in, take the drive, leave. Simplicity, in his experience, was usually the first sign that someone else had already decided what mattered and hadn't bothered telling him the rest.

A thin strip of metal between the door and its frame. A gentle pressure. A turn. The latch gave up without much of a fight.

Inside, the apartment smelled of boiled rice and medicinal alcohol. Someone lived here — a detail that hadn't made it into the briefing. A mattress on the floor. A plastic basin catching drips from a leaking pipe. Three medicine bottles lined up beside an electric fan whose blades clicked faintly with every rotation.

The drive sat on the table. Virtuoso crossed to it and stopped.

A child's drawing was pinned under a refrigerator magnet — three figures, one lying in a bed, one standing beside it, the third drawn with hands too large for its body. He looked at the medicine bottles again. Two were nearly empty. He picked up the drive, and for a moment the job became embarrassingly easy: take it, leave, and no one would ever know what else he'd seen in the process. That was how theft worked when it was done properly — the world stayed exactly as it had been, minus one thing.

He put the drive in his pocket and opened the medicine cabinet anyway. Nothing inside but gauze and a prescription folded into quarters, the name on it matching the identification card clipped to the fridge. He read the dosage, then the price on the receipt beneath it, and closed the cabinet.

A floorboard creaked behind him.

The boy in the doorway was eleven, maybe twelve, holding a kitchen knife with both hands because it was too heavy to hold with one.

"You shouldn't be here."

"No," Virtuoso agreed, looking at the knife rather than the boy.

The boy's grip didn't loosen. "Are you police?"

"Do I look like police?"

He considered this with more seriousness than the question deserved. "No."

"Good."

"Then what are you?"

There were answers that frightened people, answers that made them curious, answers that made them careless enough to lower a weapon they shouldn't lower. Virtuoso chose none of them. "Someone who got lost."

The boy's eyes flicked toward the bedroom before he could stop them, and that told Virtuoso everything the question hadn't. "Your mother?"

The boy said nothing, which was answer enough.

"She needs those." He nodded at the medicine.

He could have said he already knew. He could have said it wasn't his problem. Instead he took the drive back out of his pocket and held it up between two fingers. "Does this belong to you?"

The boy shook his head.

"Then I don't want it." He set it on the table.

The boy stared at him the way people stare at something that refuses to make sense. Virtuoso walked to the door.

"Wait."

He stopped.

"Why didn't you take it?"

There were true answers available — the job wasn't worth the trouble, someone might come looking, Ghost would ask questions he didn't feel like answering. He gave none of them.

"Bad habit," he said, and left.

Outside, rain had started collecting in the gutters. He called it in.

"The drive's gone."

A pause, then Tech's voice, dry as ever. "Define gone."

"Not with me."

"That's an unusual definition of gone."

"You'll survive." He ended the call before Tech could disagree.

The drive stayed on the table. So did the prescription. And for reasons he didn't examine too closely, standing in the rain outside a building he had every reason to walk away from, Virtuoso found himself thinking about the first thing he'd ever stolen.

It had been a watch, and the part people usually got wrong was assuming he'd needed it. He hadn't needed anything. His parents had money — food always in the refrigerator, clean clothes in the drawers, a car if he wanted to go somewhere, someone to replace whatever broke. His childhood had been comfortable enough that boredom had become an almost physical sensation, a pressure behind the sternum with nowhere to go.

The watch belonged to a drunk, loud man at a private club, gold, catching the light every time he lifted his glass. Virtuoso took it at seventeen because he wondered whether he could. He could. The answer disappointed him more than the theft satisfied him. He sold the watch two days later and spent the money on a meal he barely remembered eating.

The second theft was better. The third, better still. By the tenth he'd stopped counting, and somewhere in the run of thefts after that he'd learned that locks had personalities the way people did — some resisted out of pure stubbornness, some surrendered the moment you asked nicely, some had been designed by men who confused difficulty with security. He learned to lift a diamond without disturbing the dust in its setting, to empty a pocket without the owner feeling any lighter for it, to notice that people guarded the things they were proud of far more carefully than the things that actually mattered — which were usually sitting in plain sight, unguarded, because nobody had thought to be ashamed of them.

Money. Jewelry. Documents. Secrets. He took all of it, and somewhere between the tenth theft and the hundredth, a different kind of lesson arrived — this one from an old woman in a market, wearing a silver pendant he lifted without a second thought. He'd gone maybe twenty meters before he heard her ask a stranger, quietly, not angrily, to help her find it. She stood there with two fingers pressed to the empty place at her throat.

He went back. Slipped the pendant onto the chain while she was distracted, and walked away with nothing.

That should have been the end of it. Instead it became a boundary — one he never wrote down, never told anyone, and never once broke again in fifteen years of breaking every other rule available to him.

*Never steal from someone who needs it.*

Ghost tested that boundary the first week Virtuoso joined, though Virtuoso didn't understand it as a test until it was already over. They were in a hotel room on the fourteenth floor. Ghost put a wallet on the table.

"Take it."

He took it.

"Put it back."

He did. Ghost moved it three inches to the left. "Again." He took it again. Again. After the seventh repetition, Virtuoso set it down and didn't pick it back up.

"You could at least make this interesting."

"You already think you're interesting," Ghost said. "That's unfortunate."

"It is."

There was a photograph on the table beside the wallet that Virtuoso hadn't seen him place there — a woman, an old man, a little girl. He picked it up out of pure reflex.

Ghost's hand closed around his wrist. Not hard. Just enough.

"That stays."

Virtuoso looked at him, and understood — not from anything Ghost said, but from the specific pressure of that grip — that the exercise had never been about whether he could steal something. It had been about whether he knew, on his own, when not to. He set the photograph down. Ghost let go. Neither of them mentioned it again.

Later, when the others learned about his rule, Euphoria laughed and called it adorable. Swift asked once whether he'd ever stolen from someone poor, and when he said no, never, she'd laughed at that too and he hadn't bothered correcting her.

Now, years past all of that, he sat alone in a parked car across the street from the apartment, rain dragging sideways across the windshield in the half-second between wiper strokes.

Tech's voice came through the radio. "You're going to tell me why you abandoned the objective?"

"No."

"Good. I hate long explanations."

He kept his eyes on the lit window. "What did the drive contain?"

"Enough to make people nervous."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you're getting."

"Someone's watching the apartment."

Silence, then: "Who?"

"I don't know."

"You usually do."

"I usually steal things before they get interesting enough to be watched."

A faint click on the line — someone else joining the channel.

Maze. "Come home."

Virtuoso didn't move his eyes from the window. "That an order?"

"No."

"Good."

"Virtuoso." Something in the way Maze said his name made him look down at his own hands. There was blood under one fingernail — not his. He scraped it off against the seam of his glove.

"How did you know I was still here?"

A pause, longer than it needed to be. "You always stay too long."

The line went dead. He stared at the radio. He couldn't remember ever telling Maze that about himself, and he drove away without an answer, the apartment disappearing behind the rain, the second car parked half a block down never once entering his mirrors.

He didn't know the drive would change hands twice before dawn. He only knew that for the first time in years, a job had left something behind inside him — not guilt, which was easy, which had edges you could grab onto. This was worse. This had no edges at all. It was closer to doubt.

---

Three nights later he went back. The apartment door stood open. The medicine bottles were gone. So was the mattress. The child's drawing was still pinned to the fridge, torn in half — the enormous-handed figure ripped straight through the middle.

He picked up the larger piece and folded it once, then again, like something he intended to keep and didn't yet know why.

A noise from the bedroom. He drew his pistol, found nothing, only the fan, its blades stopped mid-turn. On the floor beside the empty bed, a brass key — old, soft-edged, the same kind Ghost used to carry — with a strip of tape wound around the bow and a single number written on it in black ink.

*14.*

He turned it over. Nothing on the back. He knew better than to assume a key opened a door; sometimes it opened a box, sometimes a memory, sometimes it was nothing at all except a way for someone to prove they'd noticed exactly what you'd touched.

He should have reported it. He put it in his pocket instead, and told himself that decision would cost him nothing, and believed, somewhere underneath that, that it already had.

That night, back at the safehouse, he emptied his pockets onto the bathroom counter — wallet, picks, phone, knife, the brass key — and looked at himself in the mirror for longer than he meant to. Nothing remarkable there. No monster. No saint. A man who had spent most of his life taking things because he could, and had built one rule on top of all that taking the way a man builds a single clean room inside a house he otherwise doesn't bother maintaining.

He picked the key back up, ran his thumb along the worn metal, and let the old rule surface again. *Never steal from someone who needs it.*

For the first time, he wondered whether the rule had ever really been about the people he refused to steal from — or whether it had only ever been about the person he wanted to see when he looked in a mirror like this one.

He put the key back in his pocket. He didn't throw it away. He didn't tell Ghost.

When he turned off the light, the mirror held nothing but dark, and the rule stayed exactly where it had always been — intact, and no longer explaining anything.
