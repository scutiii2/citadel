# V. Envy Wears a Crown

Grief, given enough time alone in a room, will eventually go looking for company, and the king's grief found its first company in a name he had not known to ask for until a spy brought it to him nearly a year after the burial: a woman on the eastern coast, a fisherman's daughter, cured of a wasting fever at eleven years old by hands that had crossed the same water his wife's illness had never been permitted to cross. The girl was twenty-three now, married, healthy, entirely unaware that a king three provinces away had begun keeping a private ledger with her name at the top of it.

He told himself, the first few times he opened that ledger, that he only wanted to understand. What had she done, at eleven, to deserve a door that had stayed shut for a queen. He sent men to watch her rather than approach her, thinking, in the private architecture of his own reasoning, that watching was a form of restraint. He watched her marry. He watched her carry a child of her own to term without complication. He watched her live the ordinary, uncounted life his wife had been refused, and something in him that had been grief for a year began, very quietly, to curdle into something with a different taste.

He did not order the men to kill her himself. This was a distinction he would repeat to himself many times afterward, in the manner of a man building a wall between what he wanted and what he had done, though the distinction did not survive contact with the fact that the men who did it were his men, paid from his treasury, acting on an anger he had spent a year cultivating in front of them like a garden he expected them to eventually harvest.

She was found in the tide flats near her home, and her death was reported, on the mainland, as a fever returned, the same fever that had once nearly taken her, come back at last to finish what it had started. Only the king knew it had not been a fever. Only the king, and the two men who had done it, one of whom did not sleep well again for the rest of his life and eventually deserted the king's service without a word of explanation, disappearing into the interior provinces and never being found, a small unresolved thread the king never bothered pulling, because a deserter's guilt was of no use to him and a deserter's silence cost him nothing.

---

It became, over the following two years, a pattern rather than an isolated cruelty, and patterns are more dangerous than single acts because they no longer require the person committing them to feel anything at all while doing it. A man in the northern hills, healed of a shattered spine at nineteen, was found at the bottom of a ravine he had climbed a hundred times without incident. A child, healed of a fever no different in shape from the one the fisherman's daughter had carried, simply disappeared from her village and was never recovered, a disappearance her parents mourned for the rest of their lives without ever learning it had a king's signature underneath it.

The king called this envy only to himself, in the very small hours, and only once. He did not allow himself the word again after that, because a man who names his own sin correctly has to either stop committing it or admit, out loud, that he has chosen not to, and the king had already decided, somewhere in the curdled center of his grief, that stopping was no longer available to him. What use was there in naming a thing you had already resolved to keep doing.

---

Bayani heard the king's name before the king heard his, carried on the same current of rumor that carries everything eventually, a story of a monarch somewhere in the east who understood what it meant to lose someone to that island and had stopped pretending the loss could simply be endured. Bayani had spent three years building a small, quiet fury out of the empty hands he'd had at his sister's grave, a fury with nowhere honest to spend itself, and when the king's men found him in a market town two provinces from his own, offering him an audience rather than an explanation, he went, telling himself he only wanted to see the face of a man who understood.

The king received him in a room with no ceremony at all, which surprised Bayani more than any show of wealth would have. He had expected a king. He found, instead, a man who looked the way Bayani felt, which was worse, in its own way, because it meant there was no distance left between them to hide inside.

"They killed my sister and my nephew," Bayani said, "with a question instead of a knife, and never told either of us what the question was."

"Mine as well," the king said. "A wife. And an answer they gave me that explained nothing and satisfied no one, least of all them, I think, though they'll never say so."

"What do you want from me?"

"The same thing I imagine you want from me. Company. And perhaps, if you're willing, hands."

Bayani did not ask, that first night, what the hands would be for. He would ask, eventually, and the king would tell him some of it, the healed who had been found, the pattern built out of grief with nowhere else to go, and Bayani would sit with that knowledge for three full days before deciding, in the same private architecture the king had once used on himself, that a woman healed while his sister died unhealed was not, in some ledger only his own grief was permitted to keep, entirely innocent either. It was not a decision he arrived at cleanly. He did not sleep well the night he made it, and he never afterward pretended, even to himself, that he had made it for a reason that would survive being spoken aloud to his sister's grave.

He was not the only one who came. Others arrived over the following seasons, the way the king's men had known they would once the rumor of a monarch who shared their exact wound had traveled far enough: a father whose daughter had been turned away for weeping too visibly at a tree line she'd never been permitted to cross a second time, a widow who had struck a beggar in a moment of exhaustion and lost a husband for it in a judgment she still could not parse the fairness of. Each of them arrived carrying the same locked door in their chest that Bayani carried, and each of them found, in the king's private ledger and the king's quiet certainty, a room that finally seemed to fit the shape of what they'd been holding.

---

The fleet took two years to build, not because ships were difficult to make, but because the king understood, by then, that thirteen ships sailing under a banner of grief would be thirteen ships full of men who might waver at the water's edge the way he himself had once knelt in black sand and begged. He needed more than grief to carry them across. He needed a story large enough to survive contact with fear, and he built one carefully out of the material his own spies had already gathered: a false priesthood hoarding a gift that belonged, by right, to everyone who suffered, a corrupt order that measured worthiness by rules no outsider had ever been permitted to see, that had killed a queen and a child and a soldier's spine and called it justice.

Whether he believed the story himself by the time he finished telling it was a question he never let himself sit with for very long. It did not matter, in the end, whether the story was true in every particular. It was true in the one particular that mattered to the men who would have to row those ships into water that ran in two directions or none: it gave their grief a villain, and a villain is a far easier thing to sail toward than a locked door with no name on the other side of it.

His spies, in the last months before the crossing, brought him one final piece of intelligence that the king received the way a man receives a gift he has stopped believing he deserves. The island's protections, whatever their true nature, drew their full strength from the light of the full moon reflected off the water that surrounded it. On the nights when no moon showed at all, the sea's judgment grew thin, the trials grew survivable by force rather than merit, and a fleet willing to row through darkness rather than wait for weather might, for the first time in the island's long memory, arrive as an army instead of a supplicant.

He chose the darkest night of the coming season. He told no one but his captains, and told even them only what they needed to steer by.

---

The night before they sailed, he stood alone at the prow of his own ship and looked out at twelve others riding low and quiet at anchor around him, thirteen in all, each one carrying men who had, in their own private ledgers, already decided that what waited for them across that water had been earned. He thought, briefly, of black sand under a child's bare feet, a dog too solemn for its size, a woman watching from a tree line with a patience he had once envied without understanding why. He did not know her name. He had never asked it. He put the memory away the way he had put away every other doubt in the two years it had taken to build this fleet, filed beside a word he had allowed himself only once, in the dark, before deciding never to use it on himself again.

By morning the moon had set entirely, and the water around his ship lay black and unlit in every direction, and the king gave the order to row.
