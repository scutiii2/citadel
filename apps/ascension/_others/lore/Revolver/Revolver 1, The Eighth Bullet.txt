# The Revolver

## The Eighth Bullet

The city had learned to fear the dark, and not for the reasons a city usually does. It wasn't the things that lived in the dark that people whispered about at closing time. It was the things that disappeared into it.

For three months, criminals had been vanishing from the streets after midnight — a man turning down an alley and never emerging, a dealer glancing over his shoulder beneath a broken streetlamp and then simply not existing between one frame of a security camera and the next. By morning the bodies were always found, and always in the place where they had vanished: a knife through the heart, a bullet between the eyes, a neck broken at an angle necks don't bend. The method changed. The result never did.

The newspapers called him a vigilante. The police called him a murderer. The underworld, which had more practice naming the things that hunted it, called him Ghost.

Eight names had once belonged to the Revolver — eight bullets in a weapon that had never been built to fire blindly. Swift. Deadeye. Tech. Euphoria. Virtuoso. Maze. Disposer. Ghost. Each had a specialty, and each had learned the same lesson in a different way: a bullet is only as good as its aim.

Ghost had been the first. Before there was a team, there had been a young man who could enter a room he had no right to enter and leave without disturbing a grain of dust in it. Later, people would argue about which of them was the most dangerous — they'd say Deadeye, who could kill from a distance most people couldn't recognize a face at; they'd say Swift, whom stopping was like trying to stop wind once it had already decided to blow; they'd say Tech, in whose hands half the city's security systems became toys, or Maze, who could walk a building once and hold every hallway and camera and locked door in his head for years. They'd even say Euphoria, whose smile could make a guard forget his own post.

The oldest members knew better. The most dangerous person in the Revolver had always been the one who could walk into a room without anyone knowing he'd entered it.

Ghost.

Who had been missing for eleven months.

---

Tech remembered the first mission the way a scar remembers the knife — not because he chose to, but because the shape stayed. Three bullets caught. Five escaped. That was the arithmetic of the night, and he had watched the footage so many times the timestamps had become a kind of scripture. 02:17:43, a door opening. 02:17:49, Swift crossing the threshold. 02:18:02, gunfire. 02:18:11, Maze gone from the feed like he'd never been in it. 02:18:15, Virtuoso shouting something the microphones didn't catch. 02:18:20, Ghost turning around to look for him.

Then static, and nothing after it.

They waited by the extraction point for six hours — an hour, then two, then four, telling themselves at each mark that the next one would bring him — before dawn forced the math on them: blood soaking through armor, bones cracked in places bones don't crack easily, and enemies combing every road within a mile. Going back for him would have meant handing over the five who'd made it out. So they left. Not as a burial. As a suspension. The Revolver had one rule that mattered more than the others — a bullet could only be replaced when it resigned, retired, or was confirmed dead — and Ghost had done none of those things. No body had surfaced. Which meant that for eleven months, on paper and in the ledger Tech kept in his skull, Ghost was still the eighth bullet, and every lead that ended in a locked door was just another day the ledger stayed open.

Even Swift stopped saying his name out loud eventually. Not because she'd decided he was dead — because hope, held long enough without anything to feed it, gets heavier than grief.

---

Tech found the first footage at 3:12 in the morning, six monitors lit around him, three cold coffees at his elbow, a small mechanical spider walking figure-eights across his keyboard because his hands needed somewhere to put the waiting.

"Got something."

No one answered. He turned. Deadeye sat by the window cleaning a rifle that was already clean. Swift was asleep on the couch with one boot still laced. Virtuoso dealt himself a hand of cards he wasn't going to finish. Euphoria read a newspaper folded so many times the print had gone soft as cloth. Disposer sharpened a utility blade that had stopped needing it an hour ago. The chair by the door was empty, the way it had been empty since Maze.

"Everyone," Tech said, louder, and this time they came.

The footage showed a street under a dead lamp. A man walked into frame, black-clad, face hidden, and stopped. Behind him, a known trafficker raised a gun. The image stuttered — a bad frame, a dropped second — and when it resolved, the trafficker was on the ground and the man in black was simply not there anymore.

Tech ran it back. Slowed it to single frames, hunting for what the eye smoothed over at speed. There: the set of a shoulder. The particular tilt of a head listening for a sound behind it. The way the left foot adjusted its weight a half-second before the body needed it to.

Swift stopped breathing before she said anything.

"No," she said, when she could.

Tech didn't answer. He didn't need to. That stance had been burned into all of them years ago — not stiff, not relaxed, something between the two that had a name only because Ghost had invented it.

"He can't be here," Swift said.

"I know," Tech said.

"Then who is it?"

He looked at the frozen image until it broke down into pixels that meant nothing at that magnification. "I don't know."

That was the answer that frightened them. Not *he's alive*. Not *he's a stranger wearing a ghost's posture*. The not-knowing, sitting in the room with them like a ninth chair.

---

They spent two days testing what they didn't want to believe. Tech ran gait comparisons, body-proportion estimates, the angle at which the stranger held a weapon against six years of recorded footage of Ghost holding one. Inconclusive, every time — and inconclusive, they all understood without saying it, was not the same as impossible.

So they watched the streets instead, and the vigilante obliged them by appearing again. And again. Every victim carried the same shape of record: murderers, traffickers, men who preyed on people smaller than themselves, names that had slipped out of court dockets the way water slips through a closed fist. Ghost had always believed a death needed a reason before he'd deliver it. Whoever this was seemed to hold the same belief, with the same precision, and that was the first thing that didn't fit — because a copy doesn't usually inherit a conscience.

The second thing that didn't fit arrived three nights later, when Maze went outside alone.

It wasn't a mission. He told no one where he was going, because there was nowhere to go — just air, just the alley behind the safe house with its broken vending machine and a stray dog curled by the drainage pipe. He walked under the streetlamp and past it, into the dark beyond, and for twelve seconds his suit kept transmitting a location to Tech's monitors.

Then it didn't.

"Where is he?" Tech was already on his feet, already moving, and the others followed him out into a street that gave them nothing back — no blood, no weapon, no sign a fight had happened at all. Only Maze's phone, face-down in the gutter, screen still lit.

They searched until the sky went gray. At 6:31 they found the body exactly where the signal had died, and there wasn't enough of it left to explain what had done this so quietly.

---

They buried Maze without much of him to bury. The Revolver didn't grieve loudly — no one had trained them for that — so instead they went quiet in six different ways. Swift stopped eating at the same table as the others. Deadeye stopped cleaning his rifle where anyone could see him do it. Virtuoso, who had never in his life walked past something valuable without taking it, stopped stealing things he didn't need. Euphoria's smile, always on a hair-trigger, went dormant. Disposer worked later than anyone thought was healthy. Tech started sleeping next to his equipment instead of a bed, as if proximity to the footage might make it confess something.

And nobody could look at the empty chair for more than a second at a time.

It was Tech who found the reflection — half a second buried in the footage from the night Maze died, a face caught in a cracked shop window across the street. He enlarged it, watched it distort, corrected the distortion, enlarged it again, and there was no more room left for doubt once he had.

Ghost. Alive. Standing less than twenty meters from where Maze had died.

Something in Tech's chest gave way, quietly, the way ice gives way — no sound until the whole surface is already gone.

"He killed him," Tech said.

Deadeye looked at the frozen frame for a long moment before he spoke. "Then we kill Ghost."

No one argued. Not yet.

---

The truth came three days later, and it came from Maze, not Ghost.

Tech traced an encrypted file Maze had accessed eleven months earlier — a file belonging to the organization that had taken their mission apart from the inside. Maze had copied the safe house's location. He had copied their movement patterns, their schedules, the exact route Ghost had taken that night. And then he had sold all of it.

The room didn't make a sound.

"Why?" Swift finally asked, to no one, because there was no one left to ask.

Tech didn't answer, because the file didn't answer either — just numbers, dates, coordinates, transactions with no motive attached to them, the way a wound doesn't come with an explanation of the knife. Ghost hadn't vanished because he'd abandoned them. He'd been sold. By one of them. The Revolver had spent eleven months hunting an enemy that had, for at least part of that time, been sitting at their own table sharpening a blade.

And underneath that answer sat another question, one the file couldn't touch: why was Ghost killing criminals now, alone, and calling it something other than what the Revolver had once called it?

---

Line had started following him before anyone noticed Line was gone.

He couldn't have said exactly when it began — maybe after Maze, maybe earlier — but he'd noticed something the others hadn't had the stillness left to notice: Ghost never killed anyone with clean hands. Not once, in three months of bodies. Which was why the couple outside the eastern district sat wrong in Line's memory like a tooth set at the wrong angle. No arrests. No warrants. No thread connecting them to the underworld at all. Five bodies had turned up that morning — three criminals, and a husband and wife who had none of the criminals' history and all of their ending.

The couple had a son. Twelve years old. He had screamed until his voice broke, and hadn't fully gotten it back since.

That was when Line understood something the others were still circling: either Ghost had made a mistake, or someone had built him a lie convincing enough that killing those two had felt, to him, like justice. Both possibilities meant the Revolver could no longer simply hunt him and call it simple.

There was also the boy to consider, because grief has a way of turning into a second heartbeat, and Line had seen enough of that particular arithmetic to recognize it early. He watched the boy from rooftops, from parked cars, from the far end of crowded streets the boy didn't know were crowded with anyone watching him in particular. He watched the anger arrive, right on schedule. He watched the boy start chasing rumors of a masked man nobody official would admit existed.

What surprised Line was what the anger didn't do. It didn't swallow him. Something else sat underneath it, patient in a way children weren't supposed to be capable of, and patience, in Line's experience, was the only kind of danger worth taking seriously.

---

The night the boy finally found Ghost, the rain came down hard enough to empty the streets on its own.

Ghost stood under a bridge, and across from him stood a man wanted for six murders with a pistol already raised. The fight took four seconds. Then the man wasn't moving anymore, and the boy stepped out from behind a concrete pillar with a knife too large for his hand and both hands shaking around the grip of it.

He charged before he'd decided to. He never reached his target — a hand caught his wrist mid-swing, and he spun to find a masked figure holding him instead, absorbing a strike that never landed.

"Don't."

"Let me go!"

"You'll die."

"I don't care."

"That's why you'll die."

The boy went still, more from the logic of it than the grip. The figure released him, and for several seconds neither of them moved, rain filling the silence between them the way it fills a gutter — indifferently, and all at once.

Then the stranger pulled off one glove. "My name is Line."

The boy's eyes went from the glove to the mask. "You're with Ghost."

"No."

"Then who are you?"

"Someone who's been watching you."

"You've been following Ghost." The boy's grip tightened on the knife. "For three weeks."

"Longer than that." A pause, deliberate. "I've been following you longer."

The boy's face changed, and it wasn't relief. "Why?"

"Because your parents are dead," Line said, and didn't soften it, because softening it would have been its own kind of lie. "And because you want the person responsible."

The boy looked away, toward the dark where Ghost had already stopped being visible. "He killed them."

"You saw a man wearing his face. That isn't the same sentence."

"I know what I saw."

"You know what you were meant to see." Line let that sit before he went on. "There are people looking for Ghost. Some want him dead. Some want him alive enough to answer questions. I want the truth, and I don't yet know if those are the same thing."

The knife lowered, not because the boy had been convinced, but because his arms had run out of the strength that anger lends for free.

"If you want revenge," Line said, "you need to survive long enough to take it properly. Not tonight. Not like this."

The boy said nothing, which Line had learned to read as its own kind of answer.

"Come with me."

"Why?"

"Because the alternative is that you die here not knowing anything, and I don't think you want that as much as you want to believe you do."

The boy followed him into the rain.

---

The safe house had no windows. That wasn't an oversight — every door required three separate forms of identification, and every camera on the property had been physically cut from any network that reached the outside world.

Tech looked up when Line came in, and his expression hardened the moment he saw who was behind him.

"What is this?"

"A possibility," Line said. "Not yet anything more."

The boy stayed near the doorway, rain still dripping off him onto a floor built to survive worse than water.

"Mask stays on in meetings," Line told him, quiet enough that it wasn't for the room. "And around anyone whose face you don't already know. You'll understand why later — for now, trust that the rule exists for a reason older than you are."

The boy nodded. Swift studied him the way she might have studied a stray dog deciding whether to approach a hand. Deadeye didn't look at him at all. Virtuoso leaned against the wall with his arms crossed, unreadable. Euphoria watched without her usual smile. Disposer kept cleaning blood off a piece of equipment as if the boy's arrival hadn't changed the shape of the evening.

It was Tech who finally spoke, eyes on the empty chair rather than the boy. "For Maze?"

"Not yet," Line said. Then, to the boy: "That depends on him."

The boy sat when the chair was offered, and for the first time since the morning he'd found his parents, someone asked him a question that wasn't about how afraid he was.

"Every bullet chooses its own name," Line said. "What's yours?"

The boy thought of the bodies in the road. Of the man who had vanished into the dark afterward, unpunished, unnamed. Of the knife in his hand — too large, too heavy, but his, the only thing in the last three weeks that had been entirely his.

"Dagger."

Line nodded and entered the name into the Revolver's records — not as a bullet yet, only as a possibility, the way a seed is entered into soil before anyone's sure it will take.

Somewhere beyond the sealed walls, on the roof of a building no one had bothered to condemn yet, Ghost stood over the city and watched a light he couldn't see go on in a room he'd never been permitted to enter.

He had heard the boy's name by other means, hours before Line brought him in.

For the first time in eleven months, Ghost did something he hadn't done since the night he was taken.

He smiled — not because the boy was safe, but because the boy was now standing exactly where Ghost had spent eleven months arranging for him to stand.
