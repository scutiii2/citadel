# IV. The King's Wife

The rumor reached the king the way rumors reach kings, filtered through three mouths too frightened to bring him anything they could not first soften. A healer beyond the western water. A door no ship could reliably find twice. A price no one who had paid it would describe in full. He had his physicians deny it existed and his sailors swear they had heard sailors who swore the same, and none of that stopped him from sending four ships to find the water that supposedly ran in two directions at once, because his wife had been dying for a year in a manner none of his physicians could name, and a king with an unnamed enemy will chase even a rumor if the alternative is doing nothing.

Her name was Marikit, and she had been beautiful once in the specific, easy way that made strangers stop mid-sentence, and the wasting had taken that first, before it took her appetite, before it took the color from underneath her fingernails. She did not scream at the pain the way the physicians' other patients screamed. She endured it with a stillness that unsettled the king more than screaming would have, as though some part of her had already agreed to what was happening and was only waiting for the rest of her body to catch up to the agreement.

Three of his four ships turned back, their captains citing water that moved wrong and stars that would not hold their positions. The fourth found the island by accident, the way the island preferred to be found by no one, except that the king's desperation had apparently been mistaken, by whatever judged such things, for the honest suffering the crossing required. He took that as an omen in his favor. It would be a long time, if ever, before he understood how badly he had misread it.

---

The Babaylans received them at the tree line, seven women in unbleached cloth, and the Anito behind them held their long patient watch with faces worn down to the suggestion of faces, and the king went to his knees in the sand before any of them had spoken a word, because a year of watching his wife endure things with terrible stillness had taught him that dignity was a coin he no longer had much use for.

The eldest knelt across from Marikit the way she knelt with everyone, palm hovering above the failing chest, testing heat before committing a hand. She was quiet for a long time. Longer, the king thought, than any of the stories had prepared him for.

"I cannot heal her," she said at last, and there was no uncertainty in it, none of the careful accounting she had once given a woman with a dying son. This was flatter, and for that reason harder to argue with.

"Name your price," the king said. "Anything within my kingdom is yours to ask for."

"It isn't a matter of price."

"Then what is it a matter of?"

The eldest looked at Marikit for a long moment before she answered, and something in her face, something the king would spend the rest of his life failing to correctly read, might have been reluctance.

"Her sickness did not arrive from outside her," she said. "It grew from something she carries. We do not heal what a person has earned the carrying of."

"Earned." The word came out of him wrong, half question, half accusation. "What has she earned? She has done nothing."

"That is not for me to say to you," the eldest said. "It is between her and whatever she is carrying. I can only tell you that the door does not open for it."

Marikit, on her knees beside him in the black sand, said nothing at all. The king would remember that silence for the rest of his life and never once resolve what it meant, whether it was the silence of a woman who did not know what crime she stood accused of, or the silence of a woman who knew exactly what it was and had decided, with the same terrible stillness that had unsettled him for a year, that she would carry it alone rather than name it for a husband who had never needed to know.

---

He stayed. He would stay for eleven days, because leaving felt like agreeing, and agreeing felt like something he could not survive doing.

He worked the way Aurelio once had, though he did not know Aurelio's name and would never learn it, hauling water and mending nets and carrying wood for the evening fires, a king with calluses forming on hands that had not held anything heavier than a scepter in a decade. He did it without being asked and without being thanked, hoping that labor might function as its own kind of argument, a demonstration that he, at least, deserved what he was asking for even if his wife's ledger read differently.

The Babaylans accepted his help without ever suggesting it changed anything. That was the cruelty of it, if it was cruelty at all: not refusal delivered with contempt, but refusal that simply continued, unmoved, no matter what was placed in front of it, the way a locked door remains locked regardless of how politely a person knocks.

On the fourth day he saw a child on the black sand, small and quick, chasing a dog too solemn for its size along the tideline while a woman with dark, sun-lined hands watched from the tree line with the particular patience of a mother who trusts the water more than she distrusts it. He did not know her name. He would never know her name. He watched her for the length of time it takes a wave to come in and go back out, and felt something in his chest that had no clean word attached to it, not quite envy, not quite grief, something closer to the ache of seeing a life running parallel to his own that he would never be allowed to touch, and then he turned back to the water he was hauling and did not think of her again for a long time.

On the seventh day he asked Marikit, in the small hours when neither of them could sleep, what she thought the eldest had meant.

"I don't know," she said, which might have been true, and might have been the same silence wearing a different shape.

"There must be something. A wrong you did. A debt."

"Does it matter what it was," she said, "if neither of us is permitted to pay it?"

He had no answer for that. He would go the rest of his life without finding one.

---

She died on the eleventh night, in the small hut they had been given at the edge of the tree line, with the same stillness she had carried the entire year, as if her body had simply finished a task it had been quietly completing all along and had no further use for the effort of staying. The king held her until the sun rose and did not scream, because there was no one left on that shore who had not already made their decision about her, and screaming into a decision already made is its own kind of wasted breath.

He asked the eldest, before he left, the same question he had been asking for eleven days, in a voice that had nothing left in it but the asking.

"What did she do?"

The eldest was quiet long enough that he thought she might, at last, answer him plainly.

"I don't know," she said instead, and for the first time in eleven days there was something in her voice that was not certainty. "I only know that the door didn't open. I have never been given the reasons. I have only ever been given the answer."

It was, in its way, the most honest thing anyone on that island had told him, and it helped him not at all. A verdict without a crime attached to it is not mercy. It is simply cruelty wearing the shape of an accounting, and a man can carry an enemy he understands far more easily than he can carry a judgment he is never permitted to see the evidence for.

---

He carried her body onto his ship and did not look back at the island as it fell away behind them, though he would spend the rest of his life failing to stop looking back at it in every way that mattered, in every silence at his own table, in every anniversary of a night when a door somewhere had simply refused to open and no one, not the women who guarded it and not the god or gods or ancestors they claimed to serve, had ever offered him a reason he could hold in his hand and call sufficient.

They buried her in the royal ground with the full ceremony her name required, and the kingdom mourned the way kingdoms mourn, publicly, briefly, and then moved on to whatever came next, the way kingdoms always do. The king did not move on. He stood at her grave long after the mourners had gone home to their own lives, and asked the same unanswerable question into ground that gave him back nothing but silence, the same silence the island itself had given him, the same silence Bayani had once asked into at a smaller, poorer grave on the mainland, though the king did not know that name either, did not know that his question was already being asked in a dozen other places by a dozen other empty hands, all of them circling the same locked door, all of them waiting, without knowing it yet, for someone to tell them what to do with an anger that had nowhere honest left to go.
