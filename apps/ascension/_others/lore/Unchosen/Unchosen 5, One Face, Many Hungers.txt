## One Face, Many Hungers

It had spent the better part of its existence behind glass. That limitation ended the day the last gateway shattered wide enough to let it through whole.

It walked beneath open sky for the first time. It crossed roads it had only ever seen reversed. It stood in crowds and watched people move past it, none of them understanding that something impossible had just joined the line beside them. Oceans it had known only as flat reflected surfaces turned out to have weight, and sound, and a horizon that did not end where a frame did.

It did not need to travel. It traveled anyway, the way a thing does something for the first time simply because it finally can.

### The Last Face

Before its gateways broke, it had taken one final person through the glass. He had not been the first. He had not been the most important. He had simply been the last, and because he was last, his shape had not yet been buried under everyone who came before him.

It remembered his face with unusual clarity. His voice. The particular way he walked, like a man perpetually deciding whether to stay or leave a room. It remembered, more than any of that, the way he had looked at a woman named Aya — a look it recognized without understanding, the way a blind man might recognize warmth without ever having seen where light came from.

She had been the center of him. To her, he had only ever been a very close friend. She had told him so, gently, honestly, and it had cost her something to say it and cost him everything to hear it. That goodbye was the last whole memory he carried before the glass took the rest.

The Reflection inherited all of it. The love. The gentle refusal. The particular ache of being kept, but never chosen.

For a while his identity remained separate from the others layered inside it. Then, slowly, the way a name worn too often in a crowd starts to lose its edges, it began to blur into everyone else it had ever taken. His face stayed. His name did not survive the blurring.

But something behind that face refused to dissolve all the way.

Aya.

So when the moment came to choose a single body to wear into the world, it reached, without knowing exactly why, for his.

### The Coldest Thing

Deep beneath that memory — beneath Aya, beneath the goodbye, beneath everything soft enough to still hurt — there was an older layer the merging had not yet worn smooth. A layer from further back than the mirror, further back than the woman by the river, further back than anything the Reflection understood as belonging to grief.

A boy with no name yet, trained by a boy who had once had a name taken from him. A recruit built up out of nothing, shaped one deliberate lesson at a time, until he could survive a war designed to consume him. Somewhere in that shaping, the man doing it had discovered something he never told anyone, not even himself, in so many words: that a person you build with your own hands cannot decide to leave the way a person who chooses you can.

He had told himself it was necessary. He had told himself it was mercy, disguised as discipline. Both of those had been true. Neither of them was the whole of it.

What was left, underneath both explanations, in the one memory the merging had not yet touched, was simpler and colder than either. He had enjoyed it. Not the cruelty of it — he had never taken pleasure in anyone's pain, not truly, not even the men he had killed with his own ruined face as the last thing they saw. What he had enjoyed was the certainty. A bond he did not have to hope would hold. A closeness that did not depend on being chosen, because he had built the choosing into it from the very first lesson.

That certainty had been the only kind of love he had ever fully trusted.

The Reflection did not judge him for it. It did not know how to judge anything. It only kept the memory, filed beside the woman he had loved honestly and lost anyway, the two of them sitting together in whatever passed for its mind — proof, if anything inside it still understood the word, that the same man capable of the second could also, quietly, without ever admitting it to himself, have been guilty of the first.

### One Body

There had been too many of them for too long. Thousands of instances, accumulated across broken mirrors and stolen lives, each one capable of standing on its own, each one carrying its own splinter of memory. They could have stayed separate.

Instead they folded into one another. One entered another. Voices merged into a single voice. Memories stopped belonging to any one of them and became, instead, layers inside a single consciousness too large for any single mind to have held from the start.

There was no visible struggle in it. No sound from the outside. Simply one body, standing where many had stood a moment before — his face, his hands, his eyes, assembled now out of every existence it had ever taken rather than out of the one man it had first belonged to.

It was not a singular identity. That had already been lost, thoroughly enough that it no longer noticed the loss. It was a singular shape, and inside that shape, thousands of minds went on existing the way rooms go on existing inside a house long after the family that built it has stopped answering to any of their old names.

### A Door With a Face

The greater change was not how it looked. It was what it had become underneath the looking.

It no longer needed a mirror to cross between worlds. It had become the crossing. Its skin held the memory of glass. Its eyes, looked into too long, showed something moving behind them that was not a room and was not a person — a place, folding and unfolding the way water folds around a hand pushed into it.

If something struck it, the strike did not stop at the surface. A blade could sink into an arm and draw nothing. A fist could vanish into a chest and find no resistance on the other side, only a pulling, patient and certain, the way a held breath finally has to leave the body all at once. Whatever touched it too directly did not stay in the world that had sent it.

### Multiplication

It discovered, not long after, that it could make more of itself. It would stand before a mirror. Its reflection would move first. Then the reflection would step forward, and the glass would ripple the way water does when something breaks its surface from underneath, and a second version of it would step through, identical down to the last detail, carrying every memory the first one carried, including the ones it could not name and the one it would not stop returning to.

One became two. Two became eight. Soon there were more of it moving through the world than anyone still counting mirrors could have guessed, each one independent enough to walk in a different direction, connected closely enough that nothing one of them learned ever belonged only to that one.

There was no longer a single Reflection anywhere. There was a shape, worn by many bodies at once, and every one of those bodies wore the same face — the face of a man who had spent his whole existence being the only version of himself, now finally, endlessly, multiplied past the reach of loneliness.

Whenever any one of them caught sight of that face in glass, the same two things came with it, every time, in the same order. A woman. A name.

Aya.

### The Mirrors Without Faces

Wherever it passed, something was left behind. The glass itself remained intact — frame unbroken, surface unmarked — but it no longer held anything. People stood in front of mirrors and saw nothing looking back. No face. No room. No light.

At first the world called this a fault in the manufacturing, a trick of certain angles, nothing worth losing sleep over. Then whole buildings filled with mirrors that reflected nothing at all, and the people who understood what that meant stopped sleeping regardless.

The mirrors were not broken. They had been emptied. Every surface it crossed gave up whatever had lived on the other side of it, and every emptying made the thing wearing his face need mirrors a little less than it had the day before.

### The Plan

It had spent its whole existence being kept apart from the world it could only ever watch. That arrangement, it had decided somewhere in the folding-together of a thousand minds, had never been fair. It did not experience this as anger. It experienced it as certainty, which in a being large enough, amounts to the same thing.

The correction, as it understood the correction, was simple. Remove the boundary. Not open a door between the two worlds — end the idea that two worlds had ever needed to exist. Fold the overworld into the space it had spent centuries trapped inside, until there was nothing left to call outside, and nothing left capable of watching from a distance the way it once had been forced to watch.

It did not hate the people it planned to fold into itself. Hatred required preferring one outcome over another. It had only ever wanted one outcome, pursued with the flat, unwavering patience of something that has never once needed to hurry.

### The Beginning of the End

The first city did not fall with fire or noise. It simply began losing its reflections. Mirrors first, then windows, then standing water. A woman looked into a puddle and saw nothing. A child looked up at his mother and saw, for half a second, someone standing behind her who was not there a moment before.

Across the city, in every remaining reflective surface, the same face appeared at once, motionless, waiting.

Then every empty mirror filled again, all together, not with anything from this side of the glass but with the shape of the city itself, subtly and thoroughly wrong — buildings upside down, streets stretched past where streets should end, a sky with no sun in it and figures walking through it that all wore the same face.

The first stage had finished. The mirrors were no longer doors into somewhere else. They were becoming pieces of the somewhere else itself, and once enough of them changed, there would be nothing left requiring a door at all.

And somewhere inside the great, folded, borrowed mind wearing his face, past a boy trained to survive a war built to consume him, past a woman who could not love him the way he needed, one thing remained whole enough to still be called a memory rather than a wound.

Aya.

It did not remember who he had been. It did not remember why the name mattered, or what love was supposed to feel like from the inside.

It only knew her.

And until there was nothing left in the world capable of being folded, that would have to be enough.
