# III. The Mother Who Would Not Be Healed

Aurelio was twenty-six when he carried his mother onto the black sand, and he had done everything the fisherman's stories said a man should do to earn the crossing, without knowing that was what he was doing. He had waited on the glass water past the point of screaming inside his own skull, because his mother's hand on his wrist had asked him to be patient the way it had asked him to be patient his whole life, and old habits are their own kind of trial. He had carried a stranger's fallen goat off the trail before he'd carried his own mother up it, tying its leg with a strip of his own shirt, losing an hour he did not have to spare, and had not once thought of it as a test. He had simply been the kind of man who stopped for a goat.

The Babaylans received him at the tree line without ceremony, the way they received everyone who reached it. Seven women in unbleached cloth. A ring of carved figures behind them, faces worn down to suggestion, watching with the patient attention of things that had been watching a very long time and had stopped expecting to be surprised.

His mother, Ligaya, was fifty-one and had been dying for two years of something no physician on the mainland had a name for, a slow unweaving that had taken her appetite first, then her sleep, then the strength in her hands, until what was left of her fit inside a blanket the way a bundle of dry reeds fits inside a fist.

The eldest of the Babaylans knelt beside her the way she knelt beside everyone, palm hovering an inch above the failing chest, testing the heat of a stove before committing a hand to it.

She was quiet for longer than she had been with anyone else that season.

"She is not sick the way you think she is sick," the eldest said at last, to Aurelio, though her eyes stayed on his mother's face.

"She's dying." His voice came out rougher than he wanted it to. "I don't need you to tell me what I already carried up that mountain."

"I'm telling you what you didn't carry up with her. She is unweaving herself on purpose. Slowly enough that you would never see the seams of it, but on purpose."

He did not understand. He would spend a long time afterward not understanding, turning the words over in the dark the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth, before the shape of it finally settled into something he could hold without dropping.

---

Ligaya was awake for the conversation that followed, though her eyes stayed closed through most of it, the way a person closes their eyes to listen more carefully rather than to sleep.

"You have a wife waiting for you somewhere," the eldest said to Aurelio, not unkindly. "Or you would have one, if you weren't spending every season of your life carrying her."

"I don't need a wife. I need my mother to live."

"She doesn't."

The bluntness of it should have felt like cruelty. It didn't, quite, and that unsettled him more than cruelty would have. Cruelty he could have argued with.

"She wants to die," the eldest went on, "not out of pain, and not out of despair, though there has been plenty of both. She wants to die because as long as she is alive and failing, you will stay beside her, and she has decided, somewhere under all the unweaving, that your life is worth more than the years she has left in hers. We cannot heal a body that has made a bargain with itself. The wanting has to come first. Hers isn't there."

"That's not a decision she gets to make for me."

"No," the eldest agreed. "It's a decision she's making for herself. You are only the reason for it."

Ligaya's hand found his in the dark of that admission, and her fingers, thin as dry twigs, closed around his with a strength he hadn't felt from her in a year.

"Aurelio." Her voice had gone soft in the particular way voices go soft right before an unforgivable honesty. "I have watched you become a man who only knows how to sit beside a sickbed. I would rather die knowing you left this island a person, than live another five years and watch you become nothing else."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said, and there was something almost like a smile in it, the first he'd seen from her in months. "It isn't. Mothers rarely are."

---

She died three nights later, on the fourth night of the full moon's waning, in a manner so unlike the wasting she'd endured for two years that it barely resembled the same illness at all. There was no final gasp, no held breath released. She simply stopped, the way a held note stops when the singer decides the song has said everything it needed to say, and Aurelio held her the rest of that night without the anger he'd expected to arrive and did not.

He did not hate the Babaylans for it, which surprised him more than the death itself. He had come prepared to hate whoever refused her. What he found instead, sitting with his mother's body on ground that hummed faintly beneath him even at rest, was a species of grief that had nowhere to aim itself, because the people who might have been its target had told him the truth as gently as truth of that shape could be told, and truth, however unbearable, does not make a good enemy.

He stayed to bury her at the tree line, in ground the Babaylans said was reserved for the deserving, which she had, in the end, earned in a way none of them had expected. He meant to leave after. He told himself that for three days, and then for a season, and then he stopped telling himself anything about leaving at all, because there was nowhere on the mainland that still felt like his to return to, and because a woman named Sinag had begun, without either of them deciding it on purpose, to sit beside him at the evening fires the Babaylans kept near the Anito, close enough that their shoulders touched.

---

Sinag had been born on the island and had never once wanted to leave it, which made Aurelio the first thing in her life she had ever wanted that the island's own order did not obviously provide for. The Babaylans took no formal vow against love, but their work asked for a kind of attention that love competed with, hands and eyes given fully to the dying could not, at the same time, be given fully to anyone else, and Sinag had watched, over the years, three of her sisters try to hold both and come away diminished by the attempt, their healing hands grown slower, their reading of the sea less sure.

She told him this plainly, the way the eldest had told his mother the truth plainly, because it was the only kind of honesty the island seemed to have patience for.

"I don't know how to want you and still be what I am," she said, one night, with the fires low and the tide doing its slow work against the black sand below them. "I've watched what happens to the ones who try."

"Then don't try to be both at once," he said. "Be what you are during the day. Let me be the thing you're allowed to want at night."

It was not an answer that solved anything. It was, in its way, the same shape of imperfect bargain his mother had made with her own illness, a division of a life into pieces small enough to survive, and he would think, much later, that this was perhaps the island's true nature bleeding into everyone who stayed on it long enough: not cruelty, not mercy, but an insistence that nothing whole could be kept, only portioned, only carried in installments a person could actually bear.

They had a daughter two years later, in a season when the moon came full three times before the child's first cry ever left her, which the older women took as an omen without ever agreeing on what it meant. They named her Aya, a small, warm word neither of them could later remember choosing on purpose, as if it had arrived already attached to her the way a name sometimes does for a child who feels, from the first look at her face, already fully herself.

She was given a dog before she could walk, a squat, solemn animal with a bark too big for its body, chosen from a litter born to one of the fishing families on the mainland and rowed across on a supply run. Aurelio named him Troy, after nothing in particular, a name that simply felt sturdy in the mouth, the way a good rope feels sturdy in the hand. The dog attached himself to the child within a week and never again allowed more than a room's distance between them, curling against her at night with the seriousness of a much larger animal standing guard.

---

There were nights, in those early years, when Aurelio stood at the tree line where his mother was buried and thought he understood, finally, the whole shape of what the island had cost him and given him in the same motion. He had lost a mother to a truth no one had wanted to hear and gained a daughter he would not have had if he'd left the island in the anger he'd arrived with. He did not know, holding Aya on those nights while Sinag tended the evening fire and the Anito kept their long, patient watch, that the island's ledger was not yet closed on his family, that grief and gift on this shore had a way of being the same entry written twice, and that somewhere on the mainland, a king was already hearing rumors of a healer no ship could reliably find, and beginning, in the private architecture of his own desperation, to decide that he would be the one man the island's terms could not refuse.
