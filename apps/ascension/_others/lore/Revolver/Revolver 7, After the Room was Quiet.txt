# Revolver 7, After the Room was Quiet

Disposer was the man they called when everyone else was finished — not when the shooting started, not when the door came down, not when the target ran. After. When the room had gone quiet and the blood had started drying under the furniture, someone had to decide what belonged to the dead, what belonged to the living, and what should never have existed in the first place. That had been his work for years, long before it had a name attached to it, and it stayed his work right up until the morning, twelve years before Ghost disappeared, that he walked into a house and found a boy screaming under a dining table.

The memory came back to him now whenever he smelled burned plastic, which was why he hated the safehouse kitchen — the refrigerator had a faulty motor that clicked, buzzed, and released a faint reek of overheated wiring every few minutes, on a schedule he could have set his watch to if he'd wanted the reminder.

He stood beside it with a screwdriver in his hand. Dagger watched him from the doorway.

"You can fix that?"

"Yes."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because it isn't broken enough."

Dagger frowned. "You fix things when they're broken."

"No." He tightened a screw. "I fix things when they become my problem."

"Is Ghost your problem?"

The screwdriver stopped, only briefly. "He's everyone's problem."

"That's not what I asked."

Disposer looked at him, and there was something in the boy's face that made twelve years fold down into half a second — blood, smoke, a kitchen table, a child's voice cracking around a word he still hadn't fully recovered. He set the screwdriver down.

"Go train."

"I already did."

"Then train again."

Dagger didn't move. "You knew me before Line did."

The room went very still. The motor clicked off.

"I know."

Dagger waited. Disposer opened the refrigerator instead of answering, cold air spilling over his hands.

"That's all you're getting."

---

He'd joined the organization believing loyalty meant obedience, and the belief hadn't started with violence — it started with his father, a factory worker who came home every evening carrying the smell of steel dust and machine oil, and who never once told his son to be good or be brave. He only ever said one thing.

*Do your part.*

Disposer had taken the phrase seriously in a way that shaped every year that followed it. When someone asked him to carry something, he carried it. When someone asked him to wait, he waited without complaint. When someone asked him to guard a door, he stood beside it until morning, and he was useful precisely because he didn't need to understand any of it first. Understanding, he believed for a long time, was something that arrived later, on its own schedule, if it arrived at all.

His first cleanup job was a warehouse — three bodies, one broken camera, blood already setting into the concrete. He was nineteen. The man training him handed over a mop.

"Don't ask what happened."

"Why?"

"Because you weren't here to investigate."

He cleaned. The blood had already worked its way into the cracks between the slabs, and he learned that night, on his knees with a brush, that removing evidence was a different and harder job than removing blood. Blood scrubbed out eventually. Memory didn't, no matter how long you worked at it.

He stayed anyway. The jobs got larger. The instructions got shorter. Then came the Revolver, and Ghost's single question the day he was brought in.

"Do you know the difference between cleaning a crime scene and erasing a person?"

He thought about it. "Yes."

"What is it?"

"A person has more places to hide."

Ghost smiled. "Good answer."

It wasn't. Disposer understood that much later. At the time, it was enough to make him feel like he'd passed something.

---

The house was supposed to be empty — that was what the briefing said. A rival organization had used it as a temporary safehouse, an operation had gone wrong, two teams had gone in and only one had come back out. Disposer arrived after the shooting, the night thick with rain and gasoline, the front door hanging from a single hinge, a ceiling fan turning slow circles above the living room with wet blades that flung small drops with every rotation.

He stepped over one body, then another. The instructions were the usual list — remove weapons, ammunition, electronics, identification, anything that could connect the dead to the operation, burn what couldn't be carried — and he worked through the house room by room, kitchen first, then the hallway, then the bedrooms.

Under a chair he found a child's shoe. Small. Blue. There should not have been a child in this house. He picked it up, held it a moment longer than the job required, and set it back down exactly where it had been.

Then he heard the screaming.

It came from beneath the dining table — a boy, twelve years old, crawling backward through blood that wasn't entirely his own, face gray with dust, hands red.

"Stay back!"

He raised his weapon out of reflex. The boy screamed again, and he lowered it, and looked around the room properly for the first time — two dead adults, a broken chair, a blood trail leading straight to the table, a photograph on the floor of a family that no longer matched the room it had been taken in. He understood most of it without needing every detail filled in.

The boy had survived. That made him evidence.

Disposer's thumb hovered over the radio's transmit button.

"Report."

"Scene status?"

He looked at the boy, who was staring at him now — not pleading yet, just staring, the way people stare at the exact moment before they decide whether pleading will do them any good.

He pressed the button. "One survivor."

Silence, and then: "Remove the problem."

The boy heard it. His expression changed before Disposer's did.

Disposer closed his eyes for a second. When he opened them the boy had gone still under the table, breathing, watching, waiting to find out what *removing the problem* was going to look like from where he was crouched.

"What's your name?"

Nothing.

"Can you walk?"

Nothing. Footsteps in the hallway, closer by the second. Seconds, not minutes, to decide between finishing the job, obeying the order, or pulling the boy out and becoming responsible for whatever came after that decision. None of the three options was clean.

He pulled the boy out. The child fought him, bit down hard enough to draw blood from his hand, and Disposer didn't let go — carried him through the back door and out into rain that hit them both at once, across the yard, down into a drainage culvert beneath the road.

"Stay here."

The boy grabbed his sleeve. "Please."

He'd heard that word from adults more times than he could count, and it had always meant negotiation. From this boy it meant something else entirely — a hand reaching into the last place trust still existed for him. Disposer took off his own jacket and wrapped it around the boy's shoulders.

"Don't come out until I return."

The boy nodded. Disposer went back inside and finished the job properly this time — bodies removed, records burned, the blood beneath the table cleaned down to bare concrete, the photograph taken with him. He left the shoe where it was.

When he came back to the culvert, the boy was gone. He searched for several minutes and found nothing, then followed a set of small footprints toward the road until they ended at a drainage ditch and stopped meaning anything at all. He stood there a long time in the rain, certain the boy had died somewhere between one step and the next.

He was wrong about that. It would take him years to learn how wrong.

---

Line had found the boy eventually — following another lead entirely, arriving after the operation, after the fire, after Disposer had already come and gone. A twelve-year-old wrapped in a wet coat, shaking under an overpass, saying almost nothing except one word.

*Ghost.*

Disposer never asked why the boy had said that name instead of any other. He told himself he hadn't known the child would survive, which was true. He told himself he'd done what he could, which was also true. He never told anyone about the culvert, which was true in the narrowest possible sense. The whole truth had always sat more comfortably with him in pieces than it ever would have whole.

---

In the present, he sat in the operations room with Tech's recovered files open in front of him — the photograph already familiar, the boy under the table, the same face grown into Dagger's. Tech hadn't needed to explain any of it twice. Disposer recognized the room, the table, the broken chair, the blue shoe still visible in the corner of the frame.

He enlarged the image. A timestamp appeared beneath it, and then a second line.

**CLEANUP OPERATIVE: DISPOSER.**

He closed the file. A knock, and Dagger came in.

"Tech said you wanted me."

"Did he?"

"Yes."

"Tech says a lot." Dagger stepped closer. "Did you know my parents?"

Disposer said nothing.

"You did."

He looked down at a small burn mark crossing the table — a dropped cigarette from years ago he'd somehow forgotten was there.

"No."

"You're lying."

"Yes." The answer surprised him more than it surprised Dagger. He stood. "Your parents were killed during an operation."

"I know."

"You weren't supposed to survive it."

"I know that too."

Dagger's voice dropped. "Did you kill them?"

"No."

"Did you try to save them?"

That one landed harder than the last. Disposer opened his mouth and found nothing behind it. Dagger waited, patient in a way he'd clearly learned from someone who understood exactly how to read a silence — hesitation was one thing, a person searching for somewhere to hide was another, and Dagger already knew the difference.

Disposer looked away. "I found you."

"Before Line?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you were twelve."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he admitted, and started for the door. Dagger stepped into his path.

"Did Ghost know?"

The air conditioner hummed for a moment before Disposer answered.

"Ghost knew enough," he said, and walked past him, and Dagger let him go this time.

---

The old operation had left one piece of evidence behind that Disposer had kept for twelve years without ever quite admitting to himself that keeping it was a choice. It sat inside a locked metal case with the other remnants of that mission — a broken watch, two spent casings, a photograph, and a small blue child's shoe. He had never opened the case after bringing it home. He opened it now.

The shoe was smaller than he remembered, the fabric stiff with old mud, a cartoon rocket printed faintly on the side. He held it, and the boy's scream came back to him whole, and underneath it, a voice he hadn't thought about in years.

Maze, two days before that house, out of nowhere.

*"Do you ever wonder who cleans up after us?"*

"That's my job," he'd said, and laughed.

"No," Maze had answered.

"What?"

"Nothing."

He'd ignored it at the time. He'd been loyal, and he'd trusted the wrong things without knowing which things those were until it was already too late to matter, and the line between those two failures was getting harder to hold apart the longer he sat with the shoe in his hand.

---

That evening he went to find Dagger training alone, a blade moving fast and controlled through his hands — Ghost had taught him well, whatever else that fact turned out to mean.

"Ghost isn't trying to make you like him."

The blade stopped. "Then what is he trying to do?"

"Make sure you survive him."

"That's supposed to make sense?"

"No."

"Then why say it?"

"Because I should have said it sooner."

Dagger waited. Disposer nearly told him everything then — the house, the bodies, the table, the culvert, the coat, the shoe — but another memory surfaced first, Maze standing in a corridor years back, watching him pack equipment for a job neither of them ever discussed afterward.

*"Some things are better left buried."*

*"Why?"*

*"Because people dig differently."*

He'd laughed at that, at the time. Now he wondered whether Maze had known more than he'd let on — not everything, but enough to have earned that particular warning.

"Your parents died because people made a choice," he said instead.

"Which people?"

"I don't know." The truth, or as much of it as he could still prove.

"Do you know who sent you?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

He could have said the name. He'd carried it long enough that saying it should have felt like setting something down.

"Not yet."

Dagger's face went through anger, confusion, disbelief, in about that order, and Disposer walked away while the blade struck the training post behind him — once, twice, again — and he kept walking anyway, because he didn't yet have anything better to offer than the walking away.

---

Before dawn he went back to the old house, or what was left of where it had been — a new building standing on the lot now, the road widened, the drainage ditch filled in and paved over without a trace. He stood across the street with his hands in his coat pockets while rain started again, and remembered the gasoline, the wet fan blades, the blood under the table, a child's hand gripping his sleeve hard enough to leave marks.

He'd spent twelve years telling himself that carrying the boy out had changed something. Maybe it had. Or maybe it had only moved the tragedy from one place to another and let him feel, falsely, like he'd been the one who stopped it.

His phone buzzed. Tech.

"Found something."

"What?"

"A file connected to the old operation."

"Who accessed it?"

A pause. "Maze."

He closed his eyes.

"Anything else?"

"Ghost."

The call ended. He stood in the rain a while longer, and for twelve years he'd believed the worst thing he'd ever done was clean that house properly. He was no longer sure that was true. Maybe the worst thing had been leaving with the boy still breathing and saying nothing about it afterward. Maybe it had been obeying long enough, and often enough, that obedience had quietly become indistinguishable from choice.

He crouched at the edge of the empty lot and found a small piece of blue plastic half-buried in the dirt — not the shoe itself, just a fragment of the cartoon rocket printed on its side. He held it in his palm and heard footsteps behind him, and turned, and found the road entirely empty.

His hand closed around the plastic. He stood there a while longer in the rain, turning over the particular shape of what frightened him now — not death, which he'd cleaned out of enough rooms to stop flinching at it. Not blood, which came out with enough scrubbing. Not even guilt, exactly, because guilt at least had a shape you could hold and name.

It was something plainer than any of that. That he had spent the better part of his life arriving after the moment that mattered — after the shooting, after the betrayal, after the bodies were already arranged on the floor, after the decision had already been made by someone standing somewhere he wasn't. Gloves on. Bag in hand. The room always cleaner when he left it than when he'd found it.

He didn't know, standing there with a broken piece of a child's shoe in his palm, whether he was looking at evidence of the past, or a reason to go back into it. He didn't throw the plastic away.

Somewhere beyond the edge of the city, past everything the Revolver's maps could account for, Ghost was still alive. Disposer didn't know that for certain. He'd stopped being sure, lately, what he knew for certain at all.
