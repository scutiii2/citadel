## The Last Honest Answer

He had kept many things in his long life. Names. Faces. Habits. The particular sound a person's voice made just before they said something they'd been holding onto too long.

He had never kept anyone the way he kept Aya.

### The Meeting

He found her on a road that had been abandoned by nearly everyone who used to travel it — a river on one side, the bones of a settlement on the other, doors that no longer had anywhere they were meant to keep out.

She was sitting beneath a tree with her hands in the water. It moved around her fingers without touching them. A dog slept against the roots beside her.

He stopped. The dog opened one eye.

She looked up at him first, before the dog fully woke — one long moment where her eyes moved over his face like a hand checking a lock it already expected to find open, and didn't. Whatever she was looking for, she didn't find it. She never mentioned it. Neither did he, though something in him noted the look the way a man notes a draft from a window he can't locate.

"You can keep walking," she said, without looking up.

"I wasn't planning to stop."

The dog opened the other eye, considered him the way a judge considers evidence, and went back to sleep.

"I suppose he disagrees," he said.

"He's usually right."

Nothing more remarkable than that happened. No prophecy. No battle. Just a conversation beside a river, and a dog too tired to bother distrusting a stranger properly.

Their roads kept overlapping after that, in the accidental way roads do until neither traveler bothers pretending the overlap is accidental anymore.

### What He Learned

She was, he came to understand, simple in exactly the places he had expected to find complications. She helped people and never asked to be repaid. She fought when fighting solved something and walked away the instant it stopped solving anything. She preferred sitting near water. She slept lightly. When she was worried her hands went still instead of restless. When she was angry she grew quieter, not louder.

Sometimes, believing no one listened, she talked to the dog about nothing — the weather, a meal, a place she wanted to see someday. He listened anyway. Underneath the nothing, he began to hear the shape of someone she never named directly. An absence with its own gravity, pulling at the way she stared across rivers sometimes, at the parts of herself she kept carefully out of every conversation.

He did not ask. He told himself that simply being present, for once, might turn out to be enough.

He was wrong, though it would take him years to learn exactly how wrong.

### The Years

They crossed deserts where she gave away water until her own strength thinned. They walked into wars without belonging to either side of them. Sometimes he vanished for weeks at a time on business he never explained, and when he returned she would look up and say, "You're alive," and he would say, "Disappointed?" and she would say, "Not particularly," and the dog would sniff him once and go back to sleep, satisfied that nothing important had changed.

He began bringing her small things. A carved scrap of wood. A coin from a country that no longer existed. A flower pressed flat between pages. Nothing valuable — he told himself he only wanted to see what her hands would do with something unexpected in them.

There was a night, on one of those crossings, when a wound he should not have survived opened him from shoulder to ribs, and by the time he understood how badly, she was already kneeling over him, her hands already moving, faster than the distance between them should have allowed. He never asked her how she'd gotten there so quickly. She never offered to explain it. He decided, because it was easier than the alternative, that he had simply misjudged how far away she'd been standing.

What he was actually collecting, he understood only much later, was her. Not objects. Reactions. The smile she tried to suppress when something struck her as ridiculous. The particular way she turned a thing over three times before deciding where it belonged.

Somewhere in all of that collecting, a future built itself inside him without his permission. A house near water. The dog asleep by a door. Her repairing something at a table while he came home from wherever a man like him went. Years passing without either of them having to leave.

None of it had happened. All of it felt, to him, like something he already owned.

He should have known better than anyone alive how dangerous that particular kind of ownership was.

### The Confession

It happened beside a dying fire, the dog asleep between them, Aya mending a tear in her sleeve with the kind of attention she gave to things that mattered less than she pretended.

"I love you," he said.

Her hands stopped. She did not look surprised. That, more than anything else that night, was the part that stayed with him.

"I know," she said.

"You knew?"

"For a while."

"And said nothing."

"What was there to say?"

He had no answer for that. She set the cloth down in her lap.

"I care about you. You're one of my closest friends."

The word landed harder than anything with an edge could have.

"My heart already belongs to someone else," she said, when he asked her to say more.

"Even after everything?"

"Yes."

"Even if he never comes back?"

"Yes."

"And if he did?"

"I'd still choose him."

He hated the man he had never met, for the length of one breath. Then he hated himself for hating someone who had done nothing but exist somewhere out of reach. She had not lied to him. She had not led him anywhere on purpose. She had simply told him, finally, the truth that had been sitting between them the entire time he chose not to see it.

"I understand," he told her.

She looked at him and knew that he did not. Not really. There was nothing else worth saying, so neither of them said it.

### What Came After

Nothing changed the next morning, which was somehow the cruelest part of all of it. The dog still wanted feeding. She still needed water. The road still went on ahead of them exactly as it had the day before.

But everything ordinary now carried a second meaning underneath it. Her smile made him wonder what it cost her. Her closeness made him wonder whether it was kindness or guilt. Every mention of the man she was still waiting for collapsed a little more of the future he'd built without her knowledge.

She began sitting farther away. Their conversations shortened. She left some mornings without telling him where. He never confronted her about any of it, because he understood exactly why she was doing it, and understanding made it worse rather than easier.

Neither of them had done anything wrong. There was simply no way through that situation where both of them kept everything they wanted to keep.

### Goodbye

It came on a gray morning, the road ahead of them empty, the dog standing between the two of them like a boundary neither had asked him to hold.

"You should go," she said.

"I don't want to."

"I know."

"I can stay. I can be your friend."

"You'll always want more than that."

He had no answer.

"I don't want to lose you," she said.

"Then don't."

"That isn't enough."

He knew she was right. He hated her for it for exactly as long as it took to start hating himself instead.

She almost said something else, then. He saw it arrive behind her eyes and saw her decide, deliberately, not to let it out — the way a person catches a door before it swings wide enough to show what's behind it. Whatever it cost her to hold that back, it cost her more than the goodbye itself seemed to.

"When we meet again," she told him, "if your feelings are gone, we can be friends again."

He almost laughed. Not because any of it was funny. Because they both already knew his feelings were not going anywhere — that a man who had outlived kingdoms and buried more names than either of them could count had never once, in all that time, learned how to let go of anything smaller than a name.

"I understand," he said.

She nodded. "Goodbye."

The dog looked back at him once before following her. Then the road took them, and he did not follow, not because he had accepted any of it, but because he understood that chasing her would only turn what he felt into something uglier than grief.

That was the last time he saw her.

### The Life That Never Happened

He had survived death before. He had survived abandonment, more than once. This was a different kind of losing — he was mourning a house that had never been built, children who had never been born, a whole shape of a life that existed nowhere except behind his own eyes, which meant there was nothing anywhere to bury.

Every morning he woke into the version of the world where none of it had happened. That, more than her rejection itself, was what wore him down to nothing. Not the loss of Aya. The loss of the future he had already, privately, moved into.

He began seeing her everywhere and never finding her anywhere. A stranger's turned head. A laugh that moved a hand the way hers moved. Every one of them collapsed back into someone else the moment they looked at him directly, and every collapse cost him a little more than the last.

He went back to what he knew. He collected. Objects, letters, artifacts, broken things — none of it helped for longer than a day, sometimes an hour, before another face from a distance brought her back and he had to start again.

He understood, finally, the one thing in his long existence he had never managed to collect. Her heart. He could keep her memory forever. He could never make it choose him.

### The Search

Grief became a search, the way grief in a man with too much time on his hands often does. He looked first for stories, then for artifacts, then for places where the rules of reality bent enough to matter. He told himself he was looking for a way back to her. That was only partly true. What he actually wanted was a version of the world where the parting had never needed to happen at all — a road that continued differently, a confession that received a different answer, a house that existed somewhere with a dog sleeping by its door.

He remembered, then, a door he had used many times before for reasons that had nothing to do with love. A dimension where nothing obeyed the same rules the overworld did. He had gone there often, once, to be rid of people he had decided did not deserve to remain in his world. He had never once gone there for himself.

He went back now, not running from anything this time, but toward something — toward the smallest, most impossible hope he had left, that somewhere past the glass there might be a version of his own story that hadn't gone this way.

He did not know, walking toward it, that the thing waiting on the other side of every mirror he had ever broken had never once stopped counting the debt he owed it.

He carried one memory in with him that he had never placed in any drawer, any chest, any vault. A fire. Her hands mending a tear. The dog breathing evenly between them. The version of her that existed just before he ruined the silence between them by telling her the truth.

He carried it past the threshold.

Something on the other side had been waiting a very long time for exactly this — a man walking in of his own will, carrying the one thing about himself he had never been willing to let go of.

It began, very quietly, to learn him.
