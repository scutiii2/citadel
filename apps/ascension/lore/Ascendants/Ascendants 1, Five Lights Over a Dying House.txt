# Five Lights Over a Dying House

The crowd came at night, because daylight would have made them look at each other's faces, and none of them wanted to see what was there.

They filled the street below the window in a shape that had no edges — not a line, not a circle, just a pressure. Someone had brought a torch, more habit than necessity, since the streetlamps still burned. Someone else had brought a child, who would remember this night for the rest of her life without ever fully understanding it. A man near the front had lost a son in the war at Corvi's Reach. A woman beside him had lost a house, and then a husband, in the resettlement that followed. They did not know each other. They would not have chosen to stand together. But grief has its own gravity, and it had pulled them all to this one door.

Inside, a man stood at the window and looked down at them, and did not move.

He had been called many names across many centuries — traveler, scholar, king, one bureaucratic title after another shed like a coat gone out of fashion — but for the last thirty years he had answered to Grin, and it was Grin's name they were chanting now, twisted into something closer to a curse.

*Weak. Coward. Madman.*

He had heard worse. He had said worse, once, about other men who had failed at smaller things.

Below him, the crowd did not know it was shouting at the being who had made the stars they navigated by. They knew only that the man who ruled them had, three days prior, stood before the Universal Committee and asked to be relieved of that rule, and that the war had not ended, and that ending was the only thing they had been promised.

He watched them the way he had once watched the birth of a species — from a great and terrible height — except that the height had worn away over the centuries he had spent among them, and what was left now was closer to level ground. He no longer saw a population. He saw a father who blamed him correctly for a boy who had died following an order that should never have been signed. He saw a woman whose home was rubble because a treaty he had brokered had bought six months of peace and cost her a street. He recognized faces in that crowd the way a man recognizes debts.

He had not meant for any of it.

Intentions, he had learned, were the cheapest thing a person could own. Everyone had them. Nobody could spend them.

Someone below shouted that without his power, Grin was nothing, and the crowd took the words up as if they had been waiting for someone to say them first, and the sound rolled up toward the window like weather.

He turned from it. In the next room, five children were sleeping, or pretending to.

---

To understand why a father was choosing, at that moment, between his children and a mob, it is necessary to go back to before there were mobs, before there was a species capable of forming one — back to a silence so complete it had never needed a name, occupied by a single presence that had also never needed one, because there was nothing yet to distinguish it from.

He had no gender. He had no body. He did not belong to the dimensions in which such things as bodies aged and failed, because those dimensions had not yet been built, and He was the one who would build them.

He made worlds the way other things breathe — continuously, without effort, without much thought given to any single exhale. Among the worlds He made was one called Scapur, and on Scapur He planted a species and gave it reason, curiosity, imagination, and the particular cruelty of being able to choose.

He expected, reasonably enough for a being who had never been wrong before, that a creature capable of understanding consequence would eventually choose to avoid the worst of them.

Humanity discovered violence instead. Not by accident — by preference, over and over, generation folding into generation, each one certain it had learned the lesson the last one missed. They fought over land none of them had made. They agreed that certain invisible lines mattered, and then died defending the agreement.

He descended to find out why.

The body He built for the purpose was unremarkable by design — a man's shape, aging convincingly, capable of hunger and boredom and the particular fatigue of standing in long lines that human bureaucracy seemed determined to invent wherever it went. He wore that body through several names before He wore it as Grin. He had the power, at any of those points, to end every war in a single afternoon.

He never used it.

A peace enforced from above, He reasoned, teaches nothing — it is only a longer leash. If humanity's wars were to end, they had to end because humanity had finally understood something, not because a god had gotten tired of watching. So He guided instead of ruling, or told Himself that was the distinction, and for a long while the distinction held.

Centuries passed the way centuries do for something that does not age — quickly, and then all at once. Humanity crossed the space between stars. It colonized. It specialized — agriculture here, industry there, medicine and trade and, inevitably, war, spread across a hundred systems instead of one. For a time He believed the scale of the thing had finally taught them proportion.

It had only taught them appetite.

Colonies became territories. Territories became empires that required, by their nature, something to expand into, and there is always something to expand into if a species is willing to stop asking who already lives there. The Intergalactic War lasted longer than most of the civilizations that started it. Entire planets became evidence.

He negotiated. He built treaties that lasted exactly as long as it took someone powerful to find them inconvenient. He stood, literally, between fleets that wanted each other destroyed, and discovered that a god's body can be shot at like anyone else's, and that being shot at does not stop a war, only delays the shooting.

Then a man risen from nowhere in particular — competent, unremarkable in the ways that mattered least, ruthless in the ways that mattered most — began ending conflicts other leaders had declared permanent. Humanity called him a peacemaker. They did not know they were praising their own maker.

By then He had lived among them so long, in so many failing bodies, that something had happened to Him that He had not designed and could not undo.

He had learned to want something for Himself.

Her name was Hail. She did not know what He was. She did not worship the thing beneath Grin's face, because she never suspected there was a thing beneath it — she loved the face, the voice, the specific and unremarkable ways he took his tea, argued badly, forgot names. He loved her back with an attention he had never once given a galaxy.

They had five children, and each child carried, folded into ordinary human bodies, a fragment of what their father was. The first Ascendants, though no one called them that yet — to Grin they were simply his, and being simply his was, for a span of years, the most extraordinary thing he had ever made.

He was a father before he was anything else, for exactly as long as Hail was alive to make that possible.

She aged. He did not. She died the way humans die, which is to say completely, and he remained, and so did the five children who would now grow up half-orphaned by a father who could not stop ruling long enough to fully raise them and could not stop grieving long enough to fully rule.

He tried both. He failed at both, more slowly than he expected, which made the failure worse, not better.

---

Which returns us to the window, and the crowd, and the five children in the next room who had been listening at the door for longer than he knew.

He had already decided, before the shouting started, that his enemies would not stop at him. A god's children are not an abstraction to men who have just been told their god is stepping down; they are a liability, or a hostage, or a symbol to be broken in public. He had watched enough species turn on their own leadership to know exactly which one this crowd would choose, given time.

So there would be no time.

Five pods were readied before dawn — vessels built for velocities no ordinary body could survive, on the theory, correct as it turned out, that these were not ordinary bodies. As the pods accelerated past what flesh could tolerate, the children's physical forms did not so much die as come apart, releasing what they truly were into a form the pods' own systems had not been built to expect and adapted to anyway, wrapping each child's raw inheritance in a shell of living light.

Grin did not watch them launch. He had wanted to. He found, in the end, that some things a father cannot make himself see twice.

The crowd had anticipated the escape. It is a peculiar feature of mobs that they are frequently smarter, collectively, than any single person in them — someone had thought to bring more than torches. Missiles rose to meet two of the five lights before they cleared the atmosphere, and found them.

Two of Grin's children ended there, within sight of the house they had been born in, in the sky above the crowd that had come to remove their father.

A third pod was struck only glancingly — enough to cripple its navigation, not enough to end it. It tumbled off its intended course into a trajectory nobody had planned and nobody could correct, carrying its passenger somewhere that was no longer anywhere in particular.

The remaining two reached their destinations. Where they struck, they struck hard enough to gouge craters into worlds untouched by anything resembling a city, and where the craters should have simply remained scars, they didn't. What poured out of the failing vessels was not blood and was not simply power — it moved through soil and ocean and empty air the way an idea moves through a population that's ready for it, and where it settled, it built.

On one such world — a small, unremarkable stone that would only much later acquire the name Earth — that inheritance became a civilization before it became anything else. The Deities did not evolve there so much as arrive there, fully formed from borrowed divinity, inheriting power without ever meeting the two children whose scattered selves had made them possible. Those two Ascendants slept beneath the world they had accidentally created, their bodies folded into the wreckage of their own pods, and would not wake for longer than any species born of them could conceive of waiting.

The third pod, the crippled one, went on falling through the dark for a span of time that had stopped mattering to the girl asleep inside it. Unlike her brothers, her body had not been destroyed by the launch — only stranded, drifting, preserved by a failing vessel that refused, against every design specification, to let its passenger die.

She had come last, and she would arrive last, and by the time she did, everything that made her arrival strange would already have happened without her.

---

Earth had, by then, stopped being merely a Deity homeland. Humanity — the same species that had first learned violence on Scapur, then carried it to a hundred stars — eventually found the planet and decided, in the practical way empires decide things, that a world this far from anywhere useful was better suited to disposal than settlement. Criminals went there. Exiles went there. People a dozen governments had each independently decided they would rather not look at again.

They arrived expecting a colony and found a species that did not need their permission to already be thriving.

The Deities could have let them die in that unfamiliar terrain, and for a while, it must be said, several of them argued for exactly that. Instead, enough of them chose otherwise — taught the stranded arrivals which plants killed and which merely made you sick, which caves held water, how to survive weather that owed nothing to any calendar a human had ever built. Fear became proximity. Proximity became something neither species had a word for yet, until it needed one.

The children of those unions were neither fully human nor fully Deity, and they did not all grow the same way. Those raised inside Deity society bent toward what their Deity parent had been, generation deepening into generation, until their descendants had a name of their own: the Enchants. Those raised inside human settlements kept more of their human shape and less of their Deity strength, but the inheritance did not vanish — it thinned, and adapted, and became, eventually, the Evolved.

Two branches. One origin, already forgotten by the time either branch had a name for itself.

Beneath all of it, two Ascendants went on sleeping, unaware they had fathered a civilization in their sleep, unaware they had a third sibling still falling toward them across a distance that had, by any human measure, stopped being measurable at all.

She came down at last in a wrecked vessel over a world that had already built two religions out of her brothers without knowing either of their names. Whoever found her found a girl — intact, breathing, entirely unremarkable to look at — and treated her the way one treats a stranger found in wreckage, which is to say with caution, and then with pity, and eventually, once what she could do became impossible to explain any other way, with something closer to worship.

She had not asked for that. She did not know, when it began, that she had two brothers sleeping somewhere beneath the ground she stood on. She did not know two others had already died in the sky above a house she would never see. She did not know her father had once been called Grin, or that Grin had once stood at a window and chosen exile for his children over safety with them, or that the choice had cost him everything it was supposed to save.

She knew only that something inside her was different from everyone around her, and that the difference could be given, the way nothing the Enchants or the Evolved possessed could be given — passed hand to hand, bond to bond, turning ordinary people into something new.

They called her the Creator, because they had no better word, and because the truth — that she was only a fragment of Him, one surviving daughter carrying a splinter of something too large to fit inside any single body — was not a truth anyone standing in front of her had the information to reach.

Somewhere, still, a man who no longer answered to any name walked among a species he had made and had once, briefly, been foolish enough to love without reservation. Somewhere beneath an ordinary-looking world, two of his children dreamed a civilization into being without ever knowing they'd done it. And somewhere in a crowd that had long since dispersed and gone back to blaming other things for its grief, no one remembered that they had once stood outside a window and driven a father into a decision he would spend the rest of unmeasurable time refusing to call a mistake, because calling it a mistake would have meant admitting there had been a better door to walk through, and there hadn't been. Only worse ones, chosen in the dark, by a man who had run out of better rooms to stand in.
