# Revolver 3, Ninety Percent

The lesson began with a lie, and Dagger knew it was a lie because Euphoria had told him, three minutes earlier, that the first rule of lying was never to make one too large. Small lies survived longer, she'd said, the way a small fire survives a wind that would kill a large one.

They sat in an empty restaurant beneath a dead neon sign, the tablecloths long since stripped, dust settled into the grooves of the wooden floor. Somewhere behind the kitchen wall a refrigerator motor clicked on and off every few minutes, the only clock either of them needed.

She set two cups between them — one coffee, one water.

"Which one is mine?"

He pointed. "Coffee."

"Why?"

"You looked at it."

"No."

"You did."

"I looked at both."

"Then the coffee."

She smiled and pushed it toward him. "Wrong."

He frowned, and studied her face — which was the exact mistake she'd spent three weeks training him out of. "Don't look for the lie," she said. "Look for what the person is protecting."

He leaned back and tried again. "You lied about the coffee."

"I did."

"Why?"

She picked up the cup. "Because you were waiting for me to."

He said nothing.

"People rarely lie about the thing you actually asked them," she said, and drank, and the lesson ended there.

He didn't know she'd chosen the restaurant because no one would come looking before midnight. He didn't know she'd chosen the cups because Ghost had once run the same trick on her, years before he knew her name, and that she'd carried it forward not as instruction but as warning.

---

Before Ghost, there had been a man who loved being admired, and Euphoria had learned that about him within the first week of learning anything about him at all. Men like him were easy — they told you what they wanted to hear because they wanted to hear themselves say it back to you in someone else's voice. He bought expensive flowers. He remembered birthdays. He opened doors and never raised his voice, and it was that last part that mattered most, because the first time he frightened her he did it smiling.

She'd been seventeen. He was older — not old enough to seem dangerous, just old enough to know exactly what frightened a young woman and how to dress that fear up as devotion.

*You're different from other girls.* He said it often enough that she learned to smile at the exact moment it required. *You're the only one who understands me.* Another smile. *You make me feel safe.* A hand closing over his wrist. *You're special.* A laugh, timed to land.

She learned, faster than she wanted to, that people rarely needed to hear the truth. They needed to hear that the version of themselves they preferred was visible to someone, and once they believed that, doors opened. So did wallets. So did bedrooms. So did secrets she hadn't asked for and didn't want but kept anyway, because keeping things was what she'd become good at.

The relationship ended without an ending — she packed a bag while he slept and left with two hundred pesos, a phone that wasn't hers, and a coat that still smelled faintly of his cologne. She kept the phone, not because she wanted him, but because it held numbers, and numbers became names, and names became opportunities, and opportunities became money, and money became food, and for a while that chain of small conversions was enough to survive on.

The first time she used charm on purpose, deliberately, as a tool rather than a reflex, she hated herself for days afterward. The second time she hated the person who'd made it necessary instead. By the twentieth she'd stopped keeping count of which direction the hatred pointed.

She learned that some people wanted to be desired, some wanted to be feared, some wanted to be forgiven, some just wanted someone to listen — and the trick, the one piece of technique that never failed her, was never to give any of them the full hundred percent. Give them ninety. Let them spend the rest of their attention reaching for the last ten themselves. Control lived in that gap, not in the giving.

She was twenty when the Revolver found her, though *found* wasn't quite the word. Ghost didn't recruit her. He tested her — a man in a hotel lobby, a photograph with no context attached to it, an instruction with even less.

"Get him to talk."

"About what?"

"You'll know."

She hated that answer, not because it was vague but because it meant he already trusted her to fill in the vagueness correctly. She sat beside the man at the bar. Twenty minutes later he was telling her about his divorce. Ten minutes after that, his gambling debt. Five minutes after that, the account in Zurich he hadn't planned on mentioning to anyone.

She walked away with everything. Ghost was waiting outside.

"You didn't ask him."

"No."

"He volunteered."

"He wanted to."

"Did you enjoy it?"

She smiled. "I enjoyed winning."

"That's not what I asked."

"Then ask better," she said, and turned toward him, and for one second something in his face almost became a smile before it decided against it.

That might have been the beginning. Or it might not have been — beginnings were hard to locate in a life that had already produced several different women wearing the same face. There had been the girl who learned to smile. The young woman who learned to survive. The thief who learned to manipulate. And then there was Euphoria, who discovered, somewhere in her first year with the team, that the Revolver hadn't wanted her ability at all.

They'd wanted her judgment. That frightened her considerably more than any mission ever had, because ability was a tool — you could hand it to someone and take it back — but judgment belonged entirely to the person using it, and there was no clean way to hand that back once it had been asked for often enough.

Years later she'd teach Dagger the same lesson, without once telling him where she'd learned it first.

---

During the Ghost hunt, Dagger started avoiding her. She noticed on the third day and said nothing about it. On the fourth, she found him in the training room hitting a heavy bag too hard and too fast, the chain above it complaining with every impact loud enough to be heard through the wall.

"You've got a problem."

He didn't stop. "No."

"You've got several. I'm choosing one."

Another punch, badly thrown. His knuckles scraped the canvas on the follow-through.

"You said Ghost is alive."

"I said there's evidence he might be."

"You knew him. You knew what he was capable of." He hit the bag again. "Then why didn't you stop him?"

The next punch landed worse than the last. She reached for his wrist and he pulled away before she got there.

"Don't."

She stopped, and there it was — not anger, something smaller and more carefully buried than that. Fear rarely announced itself outright; it changed the distance between two people, altered the speed of a reply, made one hand close while the other stayed open. She'd learned to read that years before she'd learned anything else, and she read it now without wanting to.

"Did you know he was training me for this?"

She didn't answer.

He waited. She looked at his bleeding knuckles instead of his eyes. "You're bleeding."

"I asked you a question."

"And I answered."

"No, you didn't."

"I know."

He laughed once, with no humor in it at all. "That's what you taught me. How to tell when someone's lying."

She took a clean cloth from the bench and tossed it to him. "You've been paying attention."

He caught it. "Did you know?"

She was already walking toward the door when he said her name.

For a second she nearly turned around. Instead she said, "Ghost taught all of us things he didn't intend to," and left, and she knew before the door closed behind her that the answer wasn't enough. That was exactly why she'd left before he could ask her to make it better.

---

There had been another lesson, years before Dagger, years before Ghost disappeared. Maze had been sitting at the far end of a table sorting photographs into neat piles, unhurried, the way he did everything.

"You don't like me," she'd said.

"I didn't say that."

"You don't have to."

He set another photograph down. "Do you like me?"

"That's different."

"Why?"

"Because you asked."

She'd laughed. "That's evasive."

"No. It's efficient."

She leaned across the table. "You're afraid of me."

"Should I be?"

"I didn't ask that."

"No," he agreed, and studied her the way she usually studied other people — waiting for the twitch, the pause, the misplaced word, the glance toward the door that gave a person away without their permission. Maze gave away nothing. That had irritated her more than anything else about him.

"You're hiding something."

"Everyone is."

"That's convenient."

"It is."

She reached across the table and took one of the photographs. His hand covered hers before it got there. Neither of them moved.

"You're very good at making people tell you things," he said quietly.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't praise."

"I know."

He let go. She put the photograph back, and neither of them mentioned it again. Years later, after his betrayal came apart in everyone's hands at once, she wouldn't remember the words of that exchange nearly as clearly as she remembered the way his hand had covered hers — precisely, without hesitation, as if he'd known in advance exactly where to touch.

---

The next Ghost-hunt operation sent her to a hotel in the eastern district. Dagger stayed behind. She was meeting a broker who arrived with two bodyguards and a woman in a green dress that Euphoria recognized after a beat too long — someone she'd seen once before, years ago, older now, hair cut shorter, a pale scar new beneath her jaw.

The woman sat across from her. "You don't remember me."

"I remember everyone," Euphoria said, pouring tea.

"That's another lie."

She handed over the cup. The woman didn't drink it.

"Your friend sent me."

"Which one?"

"The one who disappears."

Her fingers stopped against the saucer for a fraction of a second — long enough. The woman saw it. Of course she did.

"You mean Ghost?"

"No. Not him."

Euphoria waited.

"You're very difficult to frighten," the woman said.

"People say that."

"I don't think you're fearless. I think you learned a long time ago that fear makes people useful."

The room went quiet enough to hear the broker shift in his chair.

"Who told you to say that?"

"Nobody."

"That's disappointing."

"Why?"

"I prefer my enemies organized."

The woman laughed — a real one, unguarded — and that was what unsettled Euphoria more than anything else in the exchange. She'd spent so many years reading manufactured affection that genuine sincerity had become harder to recognize than a well-built lie.

The meeting ended without the information they'd come for. Outside, rain had started. She stood under the hotel awning and watched people run past with newspapers held over their heads, watched a man take a woman's hand as they crossed the street, and couldn't decide whether either of them loved the other, or why the not-knowing bothered her as much as it did.

Her phone buzzed. Dagger.

"You were right," he said.

"About what?"

"The hesitation."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

That erased whatever smile had started forming. "Where are you?"

"Safe."

"That's not what I asked."

Silence, long enough to count. Then: "I lied."

She closed her eyes. "Good."

"What?"

"Now you're learning." She ended the call before he could ask her to explain that either.

She stood under the awning a while longer, listening to rain hit the metal gutter above her, until another message arrived from an unknown number. One line, no signature.

*You taught him well.*

She deleted it. She told no one — not Dagger, not Ghost, not the others — and told herself there was nothing worth telling. That was the first lie in years she genuinely couldn't decide whether she believed.

---

Past midnight, back at the safehouse, she stood outside Dagger's door and listened to him breathing on the other side of it. He'd changed. Everyone did, eventually — the boy who'd once watched Ghost as though he held the answer to every question had started learning that answers could bleed just like anything else.

She opened the door a crack. He slept with one hand under the pillow, where she'd taught him to keep a knife. She almost laughed. Instead she closed the door and went to the kitchen for a glass of water her hands didn't need any help holding steady.

That should have comforted her. It didn't.

She thought about the man who'd once told her she was special, and about believing him, and about not believing him, and about the first time she'd smiled because she wanted something from someone, and the first time — she genuinely couldn't locate it anymore — she'd smiled simply because she was happy. The memories had started to overlap at the edges until she could no longer tell which one came first, or whether the order mattered anymore.

She looked at her reflection in the dark kitchen window and tried, for no reason she could name, to make herself smile. She stopped halfway through. The expression was convincing. That was the problem — she couldn't tell whether it belonged to her, or whether it was simply the ninetieth version of a performance she'd built so young she no longer remembered rehearsing it.

A car passed outside, headlights sweeping the kitchen wall and gone. She stayed where she was.

She'd spent years learning to read hesitation in everyone else — the pause before an answer, a hand withdrawn a beat too fast, a smile held one second longer than the moment required, a voice that changed shape at exactly the wrong word. She could read strangers. She could read enemies. She could read Dagger without trying, and probably still Ghost, wherever he was. But turned inward, the signal went to static — a survival instinct under a habit, a habit under a performance, a performance under something she'd once, a long time ago, been willing to call affection, and she no longer knew where any one layer ended and the next one began.

Somewhere beyond the safehouse walls, someone who understood exactly how she worked was still moving through the dark, close enough to leave a message and far enough away that she couldn't do anything about it.

She turned off the kitchen light and left the window as it was, and didn't examine too closely whether she was afraid someone might be watching, or whether some smaller, more stubborn part of her was hoping they were.
