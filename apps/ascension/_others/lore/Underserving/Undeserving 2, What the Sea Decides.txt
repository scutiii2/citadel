# II. What the Sea Decides

No map showed the island, and this was not an oversight. Sailors who came close enough to sight it described the water around it the way a man describes a wound he got while looking away, in pieces, out of order, unwilling to commit to a single account. Some said the sea there ran in two directions at once. Some said it ran in none. What was agreed on, by the handful who survived coming close and turning back, was that the island did not want to be found by accident, and it was very good at getting what it wanted.

It wanted, instead, to be found on purpose, by people willing to pay for the privilege in the one currency it accepted, which was suffering honestly carried.

Corazon carried hers wrapped in a blanket the color of old rust, against her chest, in a boat she had traded four years of saved coin to own. Her son had stopped crying somewhere around his fourth year of sickness, not because the pain had lessened but because his body had run out of the strength crying required. He was eleven now, and weighed less than he had at seven, and slept most of every day in a way that no longer resembled rest.

She had heard about the island the way everyone heard about it, from someone who had heard it from someone else, a chain of telling so long that the island itself had become less a place than a rumor with a location attached. A healer for the unhealable. A door that only opened for the deserving, whatever that word meant to whatever judged it. She had not asked what happened to the ones who tried and were found wanting. She had understood, from the way people's voices changed when they got near that part of the story, that she did not want the answer badly enough to ask for it twice.

---

The first trial did not announce itself as a trial. It arrived as weather.

The sea around the island calmed to glass three hours before dawn and held that stillness for exactly as long as a person could wait without moving, and not one second longer. Corazon learned this only because an old fisherman, toothless and uninterested in payment, told her the shape of it the night before she left the mainland, in a voice that suggested he was repeating something he'd been told rather than something he understood.

"Don't row until it stops moving," he said. "And when it stops, don't row yet either. Wait until you can't stand the waiting. Then wait a little longer than that."

She thought she understood him. She did not, not fully, until she was out on that glass water with her son's breath rasping against her collarbone and every instinct in her body screaming that stillness this complete could not last, that she should move now, before it broke.

She waited. The waiting stretched past the shape of ordinary time, the way pain stretches an hour into something with no edges. Somewhere in the middle of it she caught herself praying, not to the boy's father, who they'd carried into the ground three winters back, and not to any god she could put a name to, but to the stillness itself, as if it might be persuaded by begging the way a person could be. The moment the first syllable of that prayer left her, the water shivered.

It did not punish her. It only reminded her, gently, unmistakably, of the terms.

She stopped. She waited again, this time without asking anything of anyone, and when the water finally released her, an hour after she had stopped believing it would, it released her onto a strip of black sand that had not been visible from the boat five minutes before.

---

The second trial wore the shape of an old woman with a broken hip, lying at the base of the trail that led up from the sand, calling for help in a voice too weak to carry far.

Corazon had heard, from the same fisherman, that the path punished cruelty. She had not understood, until she was standing over that old woman with her son's weight already pulling her arms toward numbness, that the path's understanding of cruelty and her own body's understanding of what it could survive were not guaranteed to agree.

She could not carry both. The old woman needed to be carried, or half carried, up a trail steep enough that even an empty-handed traveler would have to stop twice to breathe. Her son needed her arms exactly where they already were, and every minute spent lifting a stranger was a minute her son's failing lungs spent without the small adjustments she made to his position that kept him breathing evenly. She had done the arithmetic of his survival so many times over four years that it no longer felt like calculation. It felt like instinct, the same instinct that told a mother to catch a falling child before the mind had finished registering the fall.

She looked at the old woman for four seconds. It was not a long time. It was long enough.

"I can't," she said, and heard how it sounded even as she said it, and said it anyway. "I'm sorry. I can't carry you both."

She left her water skin, which was the only thing she had to give that wouldn't cost her son anything, and she kept walking, and behind her the old woman's calling did not turn to cursing, which somehow made it worse. It only stopped, the way a held breath stops.

Corazon told herself, climbing, that the trail's judgment could not possibly be so narrow that it would ask a dying child's mother to weigh a stranger's hip against her son's next hour of breath and call the wrong answer cruelty. She told herself this with the specific, brittle conviction of someone building an argument she already suspected would not hold.

---

She reached the tree line as the sun cleared the water, and this was where the island's true keepers found her, seven women in unbleached cloth, standing in a loose half circle around a cluster of carved figures worn smooth by centuries of hands and rain. The Anito watched with faces that had been faces once and were now mostly suggestion, weathered down to the idea of eyes, the idea of a mouth. Even in daylight the carvings held a charge that made the air near them feel thicker, the way air feels before a storm that never quite arrives.

The Babaylans did not ask her name. One of them, the eldest, knelt by the blanket and drew it back from the boy's face with two fingers, and held her palm an inch above his chest without touching him, the way a person tests a stove before committing a hand to it.

"He is very sick," she said, which Corazon had already known for four years and did not need told to her again.

"Please." The word came out smaller than she meant it to. "Whatever it costs. Whatever I have left to give."

The eldest woman's eyes moved once, briefly, to the trail behind Corazon, to the empty stretch of path where an old woman was still lying, still waiting for help that had already passed her by and gone on climbing.

"The sea let you through," she said. "The path did not."

"I gave her my water."

"You did not give her yourself."

There was no cruelty in how she said it. That was the part Corazon would have carried with her, if she had lived long enough to carry anything, the flatness of it, the absence of any anger she could have pushed back against. It was not an accusation. It was simply an accounting, delivered the way a person reads a weight off a scale.

"He's a child," Corazon said. "He didn't choose to be sick. I didn't choose to have to choose."

"No," the woman agreed. "He didn't. And you did."

---

They did not force her back down the trail. They let her stay three days at the tree line, at the edge of what the Babaylans would allow, while she begged in every register a desperate woman has available to her, calm and then furious and then quiet again, and while her son's breathing grew shallower in the ways she had learned, over four years, to measure in fractions of a fraction. On the third night his breathing simply did not resume after one of its long pauses, and she did not need anyone's permission to know what that meant, and she held him until the sun came up and did not scream, because there was no one left on that trail to hear it who hadn't already made their decision about her.

She carried him back down herself. The sea let her leave. It always let people leave; the terms had never included anything about the way home.

---

What came after belonged less to her than to the people who loved her, which was its own kind of mercy, since she was no longer there to carry it.

Her husband was three years dead by then, which meant it fell to her brother, Bayani, to bury the boy beside his mother, and to stand afterward at the edge of a small crowd of people who had, over the years, made the same crossing and lost the same argument with the same trail, for reasons none of the survivors could ever agree on. A father whose daughter had wept too openly at the tree line, which the Babaylans read, for reasons never explained, as grief performed rather than grief carried. A widow who had struck a beggar on the road three towns back, in a moment of exhaustion she had confessed to no one but the sea, and which the sea, apparently, had heard anyway.

"What did she do," Bayani asked the small crowd, not really asking any of them, "that she didn't deserve to save her own son?"

No one answered him. No one could. That was the shape of the wound the island left in everyone who touched it and was found wanting: not a clean grievance with a clean villain at the bottom of it, but a question with no floor, one that a person could fall through forever without ever hitting the bottom of an answer.

He did not know, standing at that grave with his hands empty and his sister and nephew both under new earth, that this same falling would one day have company, that other hands just as empty were being made in other places by the same island, for reasons just as unexplainable, and that empty hands, given enough time and enough of each other, have a way of finding something to hold.

Out past the tree line, past the black sand, past the water that ran in two directions or none, the Anito stood watching with their worn-smooth almost-faces, indifferent to the grief they left behind them on the mainland, waiting, the way they had always waited, for the next crossing, and the next, and the one after that, each one judged by rules no one on either side of the water had ever been permitted to fully understand.
